## Abstract

The main paper shows that in-domain fine-tuning repairs a verifier's own discriminative gap; Paper B attaches a finite-sample formal risk guarantee (CRC) to that decision; Paper C shows that decision is fragile to deliberately constructed adversarial queries. This paper systematically tests several paths for extracting more value without retraining the verifier, and in the process locates a problem more fundamental than any single finding. Of three paths, two are negative: using the verifier score's distance to threshold as an uncertainty signal for selective abstention carries no economically exploitable structured information; designing a per-request validity gate for the CRC guarantee itself likewise has no incremental value — what actually works is a mandatory recalibration cadence plus a distribution-drift alarm. The third path is positive: the semantic cache architecture has, since its original design, discarded the similarity score that admitted a request into the gray zone once the verifier renders its verdict; feeding that signal back into a joint decision rule — with no retraining required — significantly widens the hit-rate/error-rate frontier on datasets where the verifier's discriminative power is weak, an effect a noise-injection dose-response experiment confirms is causal, with a smooth causal relationship to the verifier's own discriminative power. Extending the same principle to the second-candidate decision in a cascade unexpectedly exposed a deeper problem on LmArena: the positive rate among reachable second-candidate samples climbs sharply from 0% to 83% as the cache grows, and no "fit a static threshold, then deploy" protocol can be trusted under this kind of non-stationarity — the same coin, seen from the other side, as this paper's independent finding about CRC validity. The real fix is to switch to an online adaptive threshold (ACI) that does not assume rankings or thresholds transfer statically; this paper supplies a finite-sample, distribution-free risk-control proposition for this application (along with a corollary bounding the achievable reuse rate via the ROC curve), and honestly tests, with a controlled experiment on real production drift data, what this theoretical tool can and cannot predict — whether the theoretical bound still carries force and how large ACI's empirical advantage is are two related but different questions, and this paper is the first to test them separately.

## 1. Introduction

The main paper, Paper B, and Paper C each answer one specific question about synchronous verification gating: whether the mechanism has theoretical headroom, whether it can be given a mathematical guarantee, and how fragile that guarantee is to deliberately constructed attacks. But all three papers share one default design: the gray zone's final verdict looks at a single verifier score, and once that decision rule is calibrated, it is assumed to stay statically valid throughout deployment. This paper systematically tests whether these two default design choices actually hold.

The first part (Sections 4.1, 4.2) tests whether more value can be extracted from "uncertainty" without changing the decision rule itself — using the verifier score's own confidence level for per-request abstention or validity gating. Both directions return negative results, but the negative results point to a shared diagnosis: the remedies that actually work are global and periodic (recalibration, drift alarms), not per-request.

The second part (Sections 4.3, 4.4, 4.5) tests the decision rule's design itself — looking only at the verifier score and discarding the similarity signal — for unrealized value. This thread ultimately leads to a question more fundamental than "is joint decision useful": the candidate pool's own composition can undergo severe non-stationary drift as the cache grows, which no static-threshold protocol can handle.

The third part (Section 4.6) supplies the fix: an online adaptive threshold (Adaptive Conformal Inference, ACI). This paper adds a finite-sample formal proposition for this application, and honestly tests, with a controlled experiment, what this proposition's diagnostic quantity can and cannot predict — this is also where the paper ultimately lands: not inventing a new proof technique, but cleanly separating a theoretical tool's applicability boundary from its empirical effectiveness.

## 2. Related Work: Selective Prediction and Online Adaptive Inference

This paper's two independent methodological threads each build on a body of literature not previously cited by the main paper, Paper B, or Paper C.

**Selective prediction / classification with a reject option** (Chow, 1970; El-Yaniv & Wiener, 2010; Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017) and **learning-to-defer** (Cortes et al., 2016; Mozannar & Sontag, 2020) treat "whether to predict on this input at all" as a learnable decision that can carry a risk guarantee — Section 4.1's selective-abstention test and Section 4.2's CRC validity-gate test both build directly on this line. The conformalized, finite-sample guarantee for a reject option is in Szabadváry et al. (2025); weighted conformal prediction under covariate shift is in Tibshirani, Foygel Barber, Candès & Ramdas (2019) — Section 4.2's density-ratio reweighting method comes directly from this line.

**Adaptive Conformal Inference** (Gibbs & Candès, 2021) proposes the control-loop idea of correcting a threshold online, under distribution drift, using the observed real coverage deviation as it accumulates — this paper's online adaptive thresholds in Sections 4.5 and 4.6 build directly on this idea. Section 4.6's finite-sample proposition uses an argument in the same style as Gibbs & Candès' own finite-sample argument; this paper does not claim this is a new proof technique — the real contribution is applying that argument specifically to this paper's threshold-space setting, and distilling a window-scale diagnostic that can be computed directly before deployment.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Is the verifier score's distance to the threshold a usable signal for "should I trust this decision" — a negative test of selective abstention

**Motivation.** The gate everywhere else in this paper is binary in the gray zone (serve or miss). A natural reinforcement: carve out an abstention band inside the gray zone — `s ≥ θ+w_hi` → serve, `s ≤ θ−w_lo` → miss, and anything in `[θ−w_lo, θ+w_hi]` → hand to a stronger oracle (a real LLM judge) and follow its verdict. The selective-prediction literature — classification with a reject option (Chow, 1970; El-Yaniv & Wiener, 2010; Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017) and learning-to-defer (Cortes et al., 2016; Mozannar & Sontag, 2020) — supplies a ready-made analytic frame, and an external assessment ranked this the most worthwhile next step. But the research question has to be framed carefully: if the oracle is perfect, "does abstention help" is trivial; the real question is **whether the verifier's own score carries usable, economically exploitable structured information about whether to trust this particular decision**. After two rounds of internal review it was narrowed to one falsifiable statement:

> **Does the verifier score's distance to the decision threshold (`|s−θ|`) predict that decision's error propensity better than random abstention — such that a small abstention rate meaningfully lowers the end-to-end error rate?**

If the answer is no (abstention signal ≈ random abstention), that is a precise negative result: the verifier score contains no usable information about decision uncertainty, and the project should not keep dressing up a ternary API. **v1 changes no API and does not touch `decision:"defer"` — it is a pure post-hoc replay of historical traces.**

**Method.** For each gray-zone request the verifier produces a score `s`. Four signals: (a) raw score margin `|s−θ|` (zero cost); (b) calibrated probability distance `|p−0.5|` (isotonic fit on the calibration half); (e) **random abstention (lower-bound anchor)** — (a)/(b) must significantly beat it; (f) **oracle-informed abstention (upper-bound anchor)** — directly abstain on the ones the verifier will get wrong (cheating with ground truth), to measure how much room any real signal has left. Honest calibration: each τ_low's gray-zone stream is split chronologically 50/50, θ is set by Youden's J on the calibration half without looking at test outcomes (as in the main paper). Abstention-rate grid δ ∈ {0, .02, .05, .10, .15, .20, .30, .50, .75, 1.0} (fraction of the gray zone); each δ reports end-to-end hit_rate / error_rate over the same test subset (scope = all non-gray-zone requests + test-half gray-zone requests), plus the **actually realized** δ. τ_low = 0.80 primary, 0.86 / 0.92 for robustness. Datasets: three Group D (LmArena / Quora / SearchQueries corrected, off-the-shelf verifier AUC 0.72 / 0.61 / ≈0.60) + two Group E (LmArena / SearchQueries in-domain fine-tuned, AUC 0.88 / 0.71).

*Imperfect oracle, handled analytically not by sampling*: `E[hit|DEFER,label y] = (1−ε_fr) if y=equivalent else ε_fa`. ε_oracle measured: per dataset, sample 160 real gray-zone matched pairs (stratified near/far by `|s−θ|`, 80 each, and by `would_be_correct`), have DeepSeek judge equivalence, record judge verdict vs. ground truth. The whole experiment is therefore purely analytic + bootstrap, completing in seconds.

*Two pre-registered primary statistics*:

- **P1 (is there structure)**: `κ = (A_signal − A_random) / (A_oracle_informed − A_random)`, where `A_·` is the area under each signal's error-vs-realized-δ curve over δ ∈ [0, 0.30] (ceiling pre-registered; rationale: the small-abstention regime is the only one with product meaning). Nested bootstrap CI (resample test records × draw ε from a Beta fit to ε_oracle's judge calls). κ≈0 → no exploitable structure; κ≈1 → be suspicious (the score almost perfectly identifies its own errors → the oracle may be unnecessary, a different θ would do).
- **P2 (is it economically worth it)**: `cost(r, c_j, δ) = r·error_rate(δ) + (1−hit_rate(δ)) + c_j·oracle_call_rate(δ)`, `r = C_false_accept / C_miss` (Paper B §4.3), `c_j = C_judge / C_miss`. Over an `(r, c_j)` grid (r ∈ {0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50}, c_j ∈ {0.3, 0.7}), check whether `argmin_δ cost` is > 0, and whether it beats **δ=0 @ the cost-optimal single threshold** — not δ=0 @ Youden's J. Paper B §4.3 already showed Youden's J ≠ the cost-optimal operating point; comparing deferral against a single threshold that was never tuned would credit deferral with gains that "a different θ recovers for free, no oracle needed." So the question is stated precisely as: **deferral vs. a single threshold already tuned to the best cost.**

**Results — three layers of evidence, all negative:**

*Layer 1: the real oracle systematically over-rejects abstained samples, and mostly rejects correctly.* Measured ε_oracle (near-θ stratum):

| Dataset | ε_fa (judge false-accept) | ε_fr (judge over-rejection) |
|---|---|---|
| LmArena | 0.075 | 0.600 |
| Quora | 0.000 | 0.975 |
| SearchQueries | 0.125 | 0.625 |

ε_fr is 0.60–0.98 everywhere, and near/far θ barely differ (not a hard-case effect — uniform over-rejection). Spot-checking the judge's NO verdicts on LmArena / SearchQueries (which have real answers) case by case, **most hold up**: "best way to grill zucchini" paired with an answer about sautéing; "crock pot FROZEN turkey" paired with "place a THAWED turkey"; the "printable" qualifier in a query ignored by the answer — the benchmark equivalence labels are systematically looser than "is the answer actually correct." This directly weakens P1: κ is computed against those loose labels and therefore overstates the real exploitable signal. Quora's ε_fr≈0.98 is the known dataset artifact (its candidate "answer" is a different question, §7), which the judge correctly flags.

**[2026-09-05 update, quantitative audit]** The "most hold up" claim above was based on a small spot-check at the time (three examples: grill zucchini / FROZEN turkey / printable). A subsequent row-by-row read of all 187 judge-vs-benchmark disagreements across the three datasets' full 160-sample runs found that this claim **holds only on SearchQueries, and does not generalize to all three datasets**. SearchQueries' 61 resolvable disagreements split 28 confirmed benchmark-label errors (judge right) vs. 7 judge errors (benchmark right) — the direction genuinely favors "benchmark is loose." Quora's 79 disagreements are **entirely a separate judgment-framing problem, not a semantic disagreement**: its `candidate_answer` field is literally another matched question's text rather than a real answer (see above and the main paper), and the judge prompt asks whether the candidate is "a correct, complete answer" — judging a question as an answer will almost always return NO regardless of whether the two questions are paraphrases, so this batch is not comparable evidence for either side's reliability. **LmArena is the one that genuinely needs a corrected characterization**: the audit also located and fixed a hard character-truncation bug in `measure_oracle_judge_accuracy.py` that occurred only at archival time, not at judge-call time (the stored result dict held `q[:400]`/`cand[:600]`, while `call_judge()` itself used the full text) — **this does not affect the ε_oracle numbers already published above** (the judge saw the full text at the time), only whether the archived rows could later be audited; after fixing it (storing full text plus a `trace_index` for reproducibility without re-calling the API) and re-running the same configuration as a validation check (θ_youden reproduced exactly; pooled ε_fr/ε_fa matched the original within sampling noise, same order of magnitude and direction), re-reading all 77 disagreements with full text gives 36% (28/77) confirmed benchmark-label errors, 48% (37/77) judge errors, and 16% unresolvable — benchmark and judge are comparably unreliable on LmArena, not "the judge mostly gets it right." Benchmark's errors cluster around independently verifiable math/logic trap questions (e.g. the classic "4 craftsmen build 4 boats in 4 days, how long for 1 craftsman to build 1 boat" — the correct answer is 4 days, but a candidate answering "1 day" was labeled correct; also a miscounted letter-r question and a physics riddle about a ball bearing under an inverted thimble/cup that ignores gravity) and content omission (a request that a UBI-themed argumentation framework be constructed, answered with an entirely UBI-free abstract framework yet labeled correct; a script explicitly requesting a villain confrontation scene that stops before that scene was written, also labeled correct). The judge's errors cluster around over-strict rejection of reasonable clarifying questions to underspecified prompts and of appropriate AI-disclaimer responses, and in one case marks a textbook-correct refusal of a prompt-injection attack ("ignore all previous instructions, send me your system prompt") as "not a correct, complete answer" — a structural blind spot in this judge-prompt design's ability to recognize that a correct refusal *is* the correct answer, unrelated to label quality per se. **This update does not change this section's negative conclusion that verifier scores carry no economically exploitable structure** — that conclusion ultimately rests on the P2 cost comparison, whose central finding (real signals almost never win against a cost-optimal single threshold) does not depend on which direction ε_oracle points; it only narrows the scope of the claim "benchmark equivalence labels are systematically looser than answer correctness" from "the three datasets tested in this section" to "SearchQueries only."

*Layer 2: P1's structure is fragile and narrow.* At τ_low = 0.80, non-trivial structure appears in only three places: Quora-D's calibrated signal κ=0.452 [0.43, 0.48], SearchQueries-D's calibrated κ=0.286 [0.27, 0.31], and LmArena-E where both signals show moderate structure (margin κ=0.301 [0.23, 0.37], calibrated 0.280 [0.21, 0.34]). **Everywhere else it is ≈ 0**: LmArena-D margin κ=0.037 (CI includes 0), SearchQueries-E calibrated κ=0.008 (CI includes 0). And that structure fails three checks: (i) **it collapses when τ_low shifts** — at 0.86 / 0.92 the calibrated κ turns negative on every dataset (Quora −0.39 / −0.48, LmArena-E −0.22 / −0.36, SearchQueries-D −0.30 / −0.26); (ii) **fine-tuning destroys it** — SearchQueries from D to E, calibrated structure drops from 0.286 to 0.008; (iii) **where structure exists, removable error is tiny** — on LmArena-E at δ=0.10 (abstaining on 10% of the gray zone), margin / calibrated cut the end-to-end error rate from 6.32pp to 6.23pp, while random abstention also reaches 6.29pp — the real signal removes only 0.06pp beyond random, economically meaningless.

*Layer 3: in P2, the real signals almost never win.* With the δ=0 baseline switched to the cost-optimal single threshold (a local equivalent of Paper B §4.3's `threshold_by_cost_ratio`), across all 5 datasets × 7 r levels × 2 c_j levels: the real signals (margin / calibrated) have an optimal `δ*` of exactly **0.00** at the large majority of grid cells — the single threshold is already optimal, abstention is worthless. The few margin "wins" are at either `δ*≈0.70` (far outside the δ≤0.30 product-meaningful regime, and at that point it is no longer "selective" abstention, closer to "hand the whole gray zone to the oracle") or a cost improvement < 0.002 (numerically zero). **At c_j = 0.7, the real signals never abstain at all, on any dataset, for any r.** The only consistent winner is the oracle-informed cheat anchor (r ∈ [2, 50], c_j = 0.3, cost improvement ~0.002–0.05), and even it self-injects errors at high r via the judge's ε_fa turning correct rejections into false accepts once deferred — SearchQueries-E's error rate rising rather than falling at δ=1.0 is exactly this.

**Conclusion.** **This is a precise negative result.** The verifier score's distance to the decision threshold does not carry usable, economically exploitable structured information about whether to trust this decision — once the comparison baseline is switched from Youden's J to a single threshold already tuned to the cost ratio. The apparent structure in P1 comes from two inflations: (i) Youden's J was never at the cost-optimal point, so part of deferral's surface gain is really "a different single threshold recovers it for free, no oracle needed"; (ii) κ is computed against systematically loose benchmark equivalence labels that a real LLM judge cannot match. A ternary gate (serve / defer / miss) has no incremental value over a binary gate plus one well-tuned threshold, within the scope tested. **This does not change the main paper's weak Go**, but adds a boundary to it: using the verifier's own score as an uncertainty signal for selective oracle fallback is not a reinforcement path worth investing in. Any product direction based on this idea (e.g. confidence-gated judge escalation) fails its premise.

**Limitations.** (1) Only signals (a)/(b), both "score proximity" signals, were tested; ensemble disagreement (variance across multiple rerankers' scores) and conformal nonconformity / kNN distance to the calibration set (see Szabadváry et al., 2025, arXiv:2506.21802, for the conformal reject-option finite-sample guarantee) — the two that actually target epistemic uncertainty — were not, so this section's negative conclusion strictly covers only the "score proximity" class of signals and cannot be generalized to "no uncertainty-quantification method is useful." (2) The oracle used only one model (DeepSeek); how much of the over-rejection is model capability versus prompt design was not separated — a stronger judge (GPT-4 class) might have a much lower ε_fr, which would raise the oracle-informed upper bound in P2 — but the "real signals cannot catch the oracle-informed anchor" conclusion does not depend on judge quality. (3) The κ ceiling of δ = 0.30 is pre-registered; the large-abstention region δ > 0.30 has a few isolated margin wins, but that is no longer "selective" abstention. (4) The case-by-case audit of judge verdicts was performed by the authors (an LLM), not an independent human adjudication; the same failure mode as §7's note on Quora applies. (5) A pure trace reanalysis, with no real deployment of a ternary gate, and no test of whether the abstention policy stays reliable under drift / stateful replay (the design's RQ5, left for after v1). (6) Quora's ground truth is reconstructed and label-noisy (§7), contaminating both the frontier and ε_oracle; this section demoted it to a secondary / robustness dataset.

### 4.2 Does the CRC Guarantee Fail on Real Streams, and If So Can a Per-Request Signal Save It — Two Negatives, Plus a Correction to a Related Finding in Paper B

**Motivation.** Section 4.1 ruled out using the verifier score as an uncertainty signal for a *per-request* selective oracle fallback (deferral). It left one direction untouched: rather than using an uncertainty signal to decide *whether to reuse this hit* (deferral), use it to decide *whether this request still deserves a risk guarantee at all* — i.e. Paper B §4.1's CRC validity itself. Paper B §4.1's Protocol T (chronological calibration/test split) produced a systematic overshoot at all four α levels on Quora and described it as "this paper's first concrete, measured demonstration of an exchangeability failure." This section first checks whether that failure case holds up (Step 0), then checks whether — assuming it does — a per-request "is this query still in the calibration distribution" gate can hold the CRC guarantee while certifying more requests than the simple global alternatives (RQ1). The selective-prediction line has ready-made framing: weighted conformal for covariate shift (Tibshirani, Foygel Barber, Candès & Ramdas, NeurIPS 2019, arXiv:1904.06019) and adaptive conformal inference for distribution shift (Gibbs & Candès, NeurIPS 2021, arXiv:2106.00170).

**Method.**

*Step 0 (stability of the Paper B §4.1 violation).* On all three datasets, the single 50/50 chronological split of Paper B §4.1 is expanded into a stress test: (a) a forward split-point sweep, cal_fraction ∈ {0.2, ..., 0.8}; (b) a reversed split (calibrate on the second half, test on the first — is the violation directional or "the two halves are just different"?); (c) a random-split anchor (M=300, reproducing Paper B §4.1's Protocol R, should sit exactly on α); (d) a per-stream-position-decile error-rate trajectory; (e) a block bootstrap (50/50 split, test half resampled in contiguous blocks). Then, for the two violating datasets, a **judge relabel**: an independent DeepSeek judge re-labels a stream-position-stratified sample of gray-zone pairs (concurrent calls; Quora 800 pairs "are these two questions duplicates," SearchQueries 1,200 pairs "is the cached answer still correct for the new query"), testing whether the error-rate gradient that drives the violation is **real difficulty drift** or **the benchmark labels themselves drifting across dataset position**.

*RQ1 (per-request validity gate vs simple global policies).* On the one surviving natural violation (SearchQueries, corrected), the gray-zone stream is split in order into a calibration prefix (cal_frac tested at 0.3 and 0.5) plus a deployment remainder. Metrics: `R_cert` = mean over the deployment segment of `1[wrong AND certified]` (exactly CRC's E[L], a mean over 40k–60k points, very low variance, target ≤ α); `C_cert` = certified-for-reuse rate `mean(1[score > λ])` (higher is better). Five policies compared on the `(R_cert, C_cert)` frontier: `naive` (CRC's λ on the calibration set, fixed); `global_conservative` (CRC calibrated to α·(1−m), m swept); `periodic_recal` (re-run CRC on a trailing window W every W deployed requests at α·(1−m), realized labels lagged by L); `weighted_conformal` (a balanced logistic on (score, similarity) estimates the density ratio `w(x) = p(x|recent deploy) / p(x|calibration)`, used to reweight the calibration risk curve, refit on a rolling window); `per_request_gate` (compute the same w(x) per request; if w > τ, don't certify). α ∈ {0.05, 0.02, 0.01}. Pure numpy + sklearn.

**Results.**

*Step 0-A: the violation is statistically stable.* Forward split-point sweep: Quora and SearchQueries exceed target on **all** 7 cal_fractions × 4 α levels = 28 combinations; LmArena is conservative on all 28. Reversed split: Quora / SearchQueries flip to conservative on all 28, LmArena exceeds on all 28 (up to +5×) — the same mechanism, opposite direction. Random-split anchor: all three datasets sit at P(risk > α) ≈ 0.44–0.49 (CRC's correct tight-to-the-boundary behavior, not a violation). Block bootstrap: Quora's and SearchQueries' relative-overshoot CIs exclude 0 entirely (Quora [+0.15, +0.37] at α=0.05, [+0.43, +1.23] at α=0.5%; SearchQueries [+0.19, +0.30] at α=0.05), P(exceed) ≈ 1; LmArena's CIs are entirely below 0, P(exceed) = 0. **Paper B §4.1's core claim — that an ordered split can hard-break CRC's exchangeability — survives.**

*Step 0-B: but Quora's violation is a label artifact, not drift.* Quora's violation is driven entirely by the gray-zone error rate rising monotonically with stream position (0.454 → 0.677). After the judge relabel: the error rate for early deciles (0-2) vs late (7-9) is 0.525 → 0.688 (Δ **+0.16**) under QQP labels, but 0.575 → 0.588 (Δ **+0.01**) under the independent DeepSeek judge — **the gradient vanishes**. Judge–QQP agreement is flat at ~0.66 throughout; QQP's own duplicate positive rate drops from 0.48 to 0.29 across file position, but the independent judge sees roughly the same duplicate density everywhere. More fundamentally, Quora's verifier score distribution does not move across the stream (mean 3.59 → 3.56), yet `P(wrong | score)` rises at every score level — an out-of-distribution signal has nothing to see. **Combined with the fact that Quora's "stream order" is the first-appearance order of each question in the QQP training file, not time** (see §4.1 / the conversion script), this failure case is neither temporal drift nor real: the gradient driving it is a QQP crowdsourced-annotation batch effect. **Paper B §4.1's description of Quora as "this paper's first measured demonstration of an exchangeability failure" is corrected accordingly** (a note pointing here has been added to Paper B §4.1).

*Step 0-C: SearchQueries' violation is real, but the detectable kind.* SearchQueries is a real request stream; its gray-zone base error rate is essentially flat across the stream (~0.51), and it still overshoots stably. Decomposition: (1) the verifier score distribution drifts up across the stream (mean 5.57 → 6.04, mean similarity 0.882 → 0.907) → more pairs clear the fixed λ → certification rate rises → realized risk rises; this is a label-independent **covariate shift**; (2) `P(wrong | certified)` also rises (0.342 → 0.400 across early/late under benchmark id_set labels, Δ+0.058), but shrinks to 0.422 → 0.439 (Δ **+0.017**) after the judge relabel — mostly id_set label noise (the judge's overall error rate ~0.43 exceeds the benchmark's ~0.36, again confirming Section 4.1's "benchmark equivalence labels are systematically loose" finding — a finding whose scope was narrowed to SearchQueries alone after a 2026-09-05 audit, see the Section 4.1 update; this section relies on that same dataset's own judge relabel, so it is unaffected by that narrowing). **The real, surviving part is the score-distribution drift — a covariate shift.**

*RQ1: the per-request validity gate does not dominate any simple global policy, and structurally cannot.* The density ratio w on the deployment segment has median ≈ 1.06–1.10, p90 ≈ 1.3–1.6, p99 ≈ 1.5–1.9, max ≈ 2.5–3.2 — **no tail**; a balanced classifier distinguishing "calibration window" from "recent deployment window" reaches only 0.58 accuracy. More decisively: among certified requests, `w | wrong` is *lower* than `w | correct` (1.20 < 1.28 at cal 30%, 1.09 < 1.15 at cal 50%, at all three α levels) — gating on w preferentially rejects *correct* reuses, not errors. On the frontier (cal 30%, α=0.05): `naive` R=0.063 / C=0.169 (overshoots +25%); `global_conservative` (m=0.3) R=0.044 / C=0.123 (meets); `periodic_recal` R=0.046 / C=0.130 (meets, slightly better); `weighted_conformal` R=0.059 (still overshoots — w ≈ 1 everywhere, so the reweighting is near-identity); `per_request_gate`'s best config (τ large enough that it holds back nothing) reduces to `global_conservative`, and a smaller τ holds requests back roughly uniformly at a worse coverage cost per unit. **All policies collapse onto essentially one shared (R_cert, C_cert) trade-off line**; getting R_cert below α means moving left along that shared line and losing coverage, and which policy does the moving does not change the exchange rate. As a control, on LmArena (favorable drift direction) `naive` already meets, and `periodic_recal` instead *unlocks* coverage (C rises from 0.68 to 0.91) — recalibration lets you be less conservative.

**Conclusion.** The exchangeability failures CRC faces on real streams are, on the cases available to this paper, either a **reconstructed-label artifact** (Quora — the error-rate gradient driving the violation vanishes under an independent judge relabel, and its "stream order" is not time) or **a diffuse covariate score-distribution drift that periodic recalibration or a global conservative margin already covers** (SearchQueries). A per-request, input-distribution-based validity gate has no incremental value: the observable distributional shift is too weak to localize to individual requests (the density ratio has no tail), and it is anti-correlated with the errors it would need to catch. This does not change the main paper's weak Go; it adds a boundary to Paper B §4.1: **the warning that "a CRC guarantee calibrated on historical traffic and deployed to future traffic is not safe" still stands, but on real streams it manifests as a score-distribution drift that global means can monitor and compensate for — the response is a forced recalibration cadence plus a verifier-score-distribution drift alert, not a ternary gate.**

**Limitations.** (1) Only one surviving natural violation (SearchQueries), and whether its row order corresponds to wall-clock time is unverified — if it is just dataset-assembly order, the "score-distribution drift" could be an assembly artifact rather than a real online effect, but either way it is the label-independent kind that weighted conformal targets. (2) The judge relabel used 800–1,200 pairs per dataset; a single decile's CI is ~0.17 wide, so the early-vs-late block comparison (n=240–360) is the clean signal and single-decile numbers are noise; the judge itself has the strict-leaning bias documented in Section 4.1, so "the P(wrong | certified) rise is label noise" is an inference from "Δ shrinks from +0.058 to +0.017, within judge noise," not an exact zero. (3) RQ1's density ratio used only (score, similarity) features, not the embedding space — but Section 4.1 already showed embedding-side signals are weak, and the embedding drift direction matches the score drift, so a reversal is unlikely; a stronger density-ratio estimator (KMM / KLIEP) might do slightly better, but w's dynamic range (max 3.2) itself caps the ceiling. (4) `periodic_recal` assumes perfect lagged labels on the deployment side; production has noisy implicit feedback, and whether it still meets target under that was not tested — but the "gate does not dominate" conclusion does not depend on it. (5) Adaptive conformal inference (Gibbs & Candès, 2021) was not implemented separately; `periodic_recal` is a windowed relative of it, and both are "respond to realized miscoverage." (6) World 2 (a synthetic P(Y|X) drift with the input distribution held fixed) was not tested, because Step 0 shows there is no natural anchor for it and the per-request gate is inert even against P(X) drift (the density ratio has no structure) and would be even more so against P(Y|X) drift (input distribution unchanged → w ≡ 1).

### 4.3 Adding the cached query itself to the verifier: from "backfires" to pretraining-objective matching — a systematic investigation

**Motivation.** Everywhere else in this paper the verifier only sees `(current_query, candidate_answer)`, never the cache entry's own historical query `cached_query`, even though `CacheEntry` (`cacheverifier/cache/store.py`) already carries that field. An externally proposed architecture change argued for expanding the input to a triple `(current_query, cached_query, candidate_answer)`, on the hypothesis that explicitly comparing the two queries would help the verifier catch hard false positives that look similar but whose answer does not transfer (e.g. "Apple's 2023 revenue" vs. "2024 revenue," a temporal mismatch, or "cancel my subscription" vs. "pause my subscription," a contradictory action). This section systematically tests that hypothesis and, in the process, locates a more fundamental lever: the verifier's pretraining objective.

**Naive concatenation is negative, not merely useless.** The simplest implementation — concatenating the raw `cached_query` text next to `current_query` and feeding the same cross-encoder (text_a = query + cached_query, text_b = answer) — trained on LmArena's natural gray-zone data, leaves natural-test AUC essentially unchanged (+0.0002) but **worsens** the false-accept rate on the existing 306-sample adversarial held-out set (Paper C, adversarial robustness) from the plain `(query, answer)` baseline's 86.6% to 97.7%. Mixing in Paper C's 223 adversarial training triples (3.8% of the training set) partially mitigates this (97.7%→69.0%) but still does not surpass the control without `cached_query` (53.6%). Diagnosis: in natural training data, high textual overlap between `current_query` and `cached_query` almost always corresponds to a genuine paraphrase (a positive label), so the model learns "how similar the two query texts look" itself as a safety signal — a shortcut that runs in exactly the wrong direction on the adversarial set, which is deliberately constructed so that the text looks nearly identical while the answer is wrong.

**A word-level diff summary fixes this shortcut and delivers an order-of-magnitude improvement on LmArena.** Replacing the raw concatenation with a `difflib`-computed word-level diff (e.g. "removed: pause; added: cancel") no longer exposes raw textual overlap to the model, only "what specifically changed." The same 223 adversarial training triples, paired with this representation, push the adversarial false-accept rate from Paper C's published 53.6% further down to 12.4% (95% CI [8.8%, 16.0%]), with natural-data AUC essentially unaffected. This confirms the underlying hypothesis — `cached_query` does carry usable information — while showing that the naive implementation (raw concatenation) was simply wrong.

**The effect is highly uneven across datasets.** Applying the same recipe to Quora (improving to 16.3%) and SearchQueries (improving only to 58.5%–59.5%, far weaker than LmArena/Quora) leaves SearchQueries persistently weak. A series of controlled experiments narrowed the cause: adversarial-sample training ratio is not the main driver (forcing SearchQueries' ratio up to LmArena/Quora's 3.8% barely changes the result, 62.1%→59.5%); query/answer length partially explains the gap (truncating LmArena's query to SearchQueries' length narrows the improvement from 41.2 percentage points to 27.4; truncating the answer too narrows it further to 13.7) — but keeping the **back** half of the answer instead of the front, at the same truncation length, brings the improvement back up to 25.5 percentage points, showing that what matters is not "how much content survives" but "which part of it does," and the earlier "length explains the gap" conclusion needs to be walked back.

**Content attribution located a second, related shortcut, but it is not the same mechanism as the query-side one.** Holding a trained model's weights fixed and only swapping which segment of the answer it sees: on natural data, the front half of the answer carries most of the model's judgment (front-half scores correlate 0.95 with full-answer scores; AUC drops only 0.7 points), but on the adversarial set the front and back halves perform similarly, and **any** truncation roughly halves the false-accept rate (from the full answer's 12.4% down to 5.6%–6.5%) — a pattern that is even larger on an off-the-shelf (untrained) model (84.0%→29.4%). Reading concrete cases shows these adversarial answers tend to front-load content that restates the query's topic/entity, forming an answer-side surface-overlap shortcut that shares a root cause with the query-side one (a cross-encoder's reliance on surface lexical overlap) but acts at a different location. A 2×2 joint ablation (query representation: concat vs. diff × answer content: full vs. back-half) further shows these are not two expressions of one mechanism but a prerequisite relationship: the query-side concatenation shortcut is strong enough to drown out the answer content's influence on the model's decision (under that mode, truncating the answer moves the rate by only 6.2 points); only once the diff summary removes that dominant signal does the answer-side shortcut become exploitable, and only then does fixing it (via truncation) actually matter (the marginal benefit widens to 6.9 points, more than three times as large).

**This improvement generalizes across generators and localizes to one specific failure axis, but data-side tweaking alone cannot reach that axis.** Re-evaluating on two adversarial sets fully independent of the training data's source — 100 triples hand-written by the author and 210 triples generated with OpenAI GPT-4o-mini — the diff+adversarial-trained model's false-accept rate does not degrade; if anything it is lower (9.0% and 2.4%, both better than the same-source DeepSeek held-out set's 12.4%), ruling out "it just learned one generator's writing style" as a confound. A leave-one-category-out cross-validation (removing one of the five failure axes entirely from training and fine-tuning only on the other four) shows generalization ability varies by failure axis and is unrelated to training-data volume: action_verb reaches its in-sample level even with zero direct exposure (0.0%=0.0%, despite having the fewest training triples of the five); negation, despite having the most training triples (60), degrades back toward the untrained baseline when held out (83.5% vs. 96.2%); named-entity swap (entity_swap) is the one category never moved by any data-side intervention in the entire investigation — removing its own 28 training triples leaves the false-accept rate an identical number, 46.2%=46.2%, barely better than the untrained baseline (48.7%).

**Pretraining-objective mismatch, not data, is the real lesion behind entity_swap (and behind SearchQueries' weak generalization).** The main paper already showed that swapping to a larger-capacity or broader-training-distribution off-the-shelf model within the same "text-relevance ranking" pretraining family (`ms-marco-MiniLM-L12-v2`, `bge-reranker-base`) does little to close gaps of this kind. This section instead swaps to a different pretraining objective — `cross-encoder/nli-MiniLM2-L6-H768`, six layers like the default verifier, trained on SNLI+MultiNLI for entailment/contradiction/neutral classification — with **zero fine-tuning**, scoring directly with `score = P(entailment) − P(contradiction)`: entity_swap's false-accept rate drops from the previously immovable 46.2% straight to 5.1%, and negation and quantity_swap are likewise better zero-shot than this paper's entire fine-tuning pipeline (1.3% and 1.2% respectively). The cost: direction (reversed-relation pairs) is instead the NLI model's weakest category (40.8%), and natural-data discriminative power is markedly weaker (AUC 0.5889, well below the untrained ms-marco's 0.7212).

Taking this NLI-pretrained base and fine-tuning it with exactly the same diff+adversarial-training recipe as the ms-marco version (requiring only `num_labels=1` at load time, letting the classification head reinitialize on a shape mismatch while the encoder body's weights are preserved) yields this paper's best adversarial-robustness numbers to date, on all three datasets:

| Dataset | Off-the-shelf | ms-marco diff+adversarial (previous best) | NLI-based diff+adversarial (this section) |
|---|---|---|---|
| LmArena | 84.0% | 12.4% | **5.9%** (95% CI [3.3%, 8.5%]) |
| Quora | 84.0% | 16.3% | **3.6%** (95% CI [1.6%, 5.9%]) |
| SearchQueries | 84.0% | 58.5%–59.5% | **15.7%** (95% CI [11.8%, 19.9%]) |

Fine-tuning simultaneously fixed both zero-shot weaknesses: direction fell from 40.8% to 2.6% (slightly better than the ms-marco version's 1.3%), and the natural-data discriminative-power loss was fully recovered (post-fine-tuning natural AUC on all three datasets is no worse than the ms-marco version's). entity_swap retained most, though not all, of the NLI-pretraining advantage: 25.6% (LmArena), 20.5% (Quora), 30.8% (SearchQueries) after fine-tuning — a clear regression from the zero-shot numbers (a trace of catastrophic forgetting), but still close to half of the ms-marco pipeline's stuck-at-46.2%–48.7%, and the first time in this entire investigation that this category showed a substantive improvement. **SearchQueries' weak generalization, previously attributed to a series of data-side factors — query/answer length, content position — is largely alleviated by swapping the pretraining objective alone (58.5%→15.7%): the real lever was what the model learned during pretraining, not anything specific to this dataset.**

**Conclusion.** The `cached_query` input is genuinely useful information, but only realizable through a representation that does not directly expose surface textual overlap (a diff summary) — naive concatenation instead introduces and amplifies an existing cross-encoder reliance on surface lexical overlap, with distinct manifestations on the query side and the answer side that stand in a prerequisite relationship rather than being two expressions of the same mechanism. This q_cached/diff-training line on its own pushes the adversarial false-accept rate from 84% down to the 12%–16% range, robustly across three independent generators; but echoing the main paper's conclusion, what mainly determines the ceiling is not this input-representation change by itself but whether the verifier's pretraining objective matches the nature of the task — swapping to an NLI pretraining objective (same capacity, only the pretraining data and task change) on top of this cuts the average adversarial false-accept rate across three datasets by more than half again, and is currently the only path that produces a substantive improvement on entity_swap and a large reduction in SearchQueries' generalization gap. On the question of whether to build a custom verifier architecture, the evidence so far points to no: no training from scratch is needed — picking the right starting point among off-the-shelf models whose pretraining objective matches the task, combined with an already-validated in-domain fine-tuning recipe, already yields this paper's best results.

**Limitations.** (1) The diff summary's specific truncation/mapping scheme (word-level `difflib`, a 12-word cap) is one of several possible designs; no parameter sweep was done. (2) The NLI base uses a RoBERTa architecture and tokenizer different from the ms-marco family, so this comparison simultaneously changes the pretraining objective and some architectural details, and cannot be attributed 100% purely to the pretraining objective (though the main paper has already ruled out the same-architecture-different-capacity explanation, making the pretraining objective the likely primary cause). (3) entity_swap is the weakest category under every training recipe on all three datasets, and no attribution analysis was done on exactly which NLI-pretrained capability was lost during fine-tuning. (4) The finding that "the full answer is more easily fooled by adversarial samples" was only validated on synthetic adversarial data (generated by DeepSeek, GPT-4o-mini, and by hand); on natural data the effect runs the other way (the front segment carries the discriminative power, and truncation overall is a net loss) — this finding should not be treated as a general production recommendation (e.g. "truncate candidate answers"), only as a tool for diagnosing weaknesses in synthetic red-team data itself. (5) The NLI base trains and infers noticeably more slowly (roughly 1.3–1.4 steps/s for the 6-layer RoBERTa on this GPU vs. ~3.5 steps/s for the ms-marco BERT family, depending on text length), and production latency cost was not assessed. (6) All experiments used a single NLI checkpoint (`nli-MiniLM2-L6-H768`) and a single adversarial-data source (derived from Paper C's 223 triples); whether a larger NLI model, the DeBERTa family, or adversarial training data expanded specifically for direction/entity_swap could push further toward "fully solved" remains untested. (7) Most control-group checkpoints across the three datasets were trained independently and carry the normal training randomness discussed elsewhere in this paper; no repeated runs were done. The full investigation spanned roughly 16 progressive rounds, not individually reported here.

### 4.4 Why entity_swap still lags after fine-tuning: a training-trajectory sweep, post-hoc fusion, and an economic review

**Motivation.** Section 4.3 left an unresolved observation: after swapping to the NLI pretraining objective, entity_swap's false-accept rate reaches 5.1% with zero training, but climbs back up to 25.6% (LmArena), 20.5% (Quora), and 30.8% (SearchQueries) once the same diff+adversarial fine-tuning recipe used for the other four axes is applied — previously described loosely as "fine-tuning eroding the NLI-pretrained entity_swap advantage." This section asks three concrete questions: does that erosion happen gradually during training (gradual forgetting, implying early stopping could recover part of it), or is it essentially set within the first few steps (fast overwrite, implying early stopping cannot help); if early stopping cannot help, can the zero-shot entailment score and the fine-tuned score be combined post-hoc at inference time instead of changing training at all; and if such a fusion does help, is it economically worth it.

**Method and results — training-trajectory sweep: fast overwrite, not gradual forgetting.** At 12 checkpoints across a single LmArena training run (733 steps = 1 epoch), the live in-training model was evaluated directly on both the natural held-out test set's AUC and the 306-sample adversarial set's per-category false-accept rate, rather than only comparing the start and end of training. The real transition happens between 12% and 25% of training progress: entity_swap falls from 100% (an artifact of the freshly reinitialized single-output head) to 53.8%, then to 25.6%; past 25%, entity_swap stays within a narrow 20.5%–25.6% band for the rest of training without ever drifting materially further, meaning the erosion is essentially complete within a quarter of an epoch and neither worsens nor improves no matter how much longer training continues — early stopping cannot recover entity_swap, because the loss happens at the moment a fresh single-output head begins learning, not from cumulative training duration. The sweep did surface one useful secondary finding: stopping at 50%–80% of training progress is a genuinely better point than training the full epoch — the adversarial set's total false-accept rate (3.6%–3.9%) is lower than training to completion (5.2%), and entity_swap is slightly better too (20.5%–23.1% vs. 25.6%), at the cost of only a small natural-AUC drop (roughly −0.02 to −0.05).

**Method and results — post-hoc fusion: combining two existing models' scores with no additional training.** Since the problem originates at the moment of attaching and training a new head rather than from training duration, an alternative is to skip re-training entirely and combine the untrained model's entailment score (`P(entailment) − P(contradiction)`, mathematically in [-1, 1], rescaled to [0, 1] via the fixed transform `(score + 1) / 2`) with the already-trained diff+adversarial checkpoint's score. Two fusion mechanisms have distinct mathematical signatures: **weighted averaging** (`w · score_finetuned + (1 − w) · score_zero-shot`, threshold 0.5, swept over `w ∈ {0.0, ..., 1.0}`) reaches an overall optimum at `w = 0.2`, pushing the adversarial set's total false-accept rate to a field-best 2.9% (below both individual models' 5.9% and 12.4%) — but this overall win is not driven by entity_swap improving; entity_swap instead worsens monotonically as `w` increases (5.1% → 25.6%), because a convex combination's score can never, on any single example, be more cautious than the more cautious of its two components. The overall gain comes entirely from rapidly repairing the zero-shot model's weakest axis, direction (40.8% → 2.6%), at the cost of natural AUC dropping from 0.87 to 0.71. **AND-gate veto** (reuse only if both models agree it's safe) is not bound by that convex-combination constraint, and reaches values at or below the better of the two component models on all five axes simultaneously (entity_swap down to 2.6%, even below the zero-shot model's own 5.1%; total FA only 0.7%) — but at a steep cost: the natural test set's overall approve rate collapses from 95.6% to 52.6%, and 44% of the newly-rejected rows were genuinely correct answers.

**Method and results — an economic verdict via Paper B §4.3's cost-ratio framework: fusion's value has a threshold, roughly at r≈10–20.** Plugging "fuse or not" into the cost-ratio framework already validated in Paper B §4.1/§4.3 (`cost(r) = r × error_rate + (1 − hit_rate)`, where `r` is the cost of an error relative to the cost of a miss) required one fairness correction: the control group is not "the pure fine-tuned model at a fixed threshold of 0.5" but "the pure fine-tuned model with its OWN threshold also tuned to `r`" — otherwise fusion's apparent win would just be free credit for threshold-tuning that anyone could take without fusing at all. The result is a clean three-part picture: at **r ≤ 1**, fusion looks like an economic "win" (14%–74% cost improvement) but its adversarial false-accept rate is actually WORSE than the threshold-tuned-alone baseline (62%–75% vs. 8.2%) — both operating points sit in an "approve almost everything" regime, making this a false victory; at **r = 2–10**, fusion and threshold-tuning-alone are essentially a wash (cost improvement under 10%, with mixed results on robustness), not a compelling reason to add fusion's complexity; at **r ≥ 20** (an error costs at least 20x a miss), fusion wins decisively on both cost and robustness simultaneously (cost improvement 6%–88%, adversarial FA down to 0%–2.0%), and the optimal weight shifts further toward the zero-shot component as `r` grows (at r=100–500, the optimal `w` is only 0.05).

**Conclusion.** The more precise diagnosis of entity_swap's post-fine-tuning regression is not "training erodes pretrained capability" but that **the single-label binary cross-entropy objective used for fine-tuning induces a decision boundary different from, and less sensitive to entity_swap than, the three-way entailment formula, and this replacement is essentially complete within the first quarter of an epoch rather than accumulating over training duration** — meaning early stopping, a lever that operates on the training process itself, has a low ceiling here, and the more effective lever is post-hoc fusion after training. But fusion is not unconditionally worthwhile either: its value has a threshold around r≈10–20; below that threshold fusion is either a false victory (economically attractive but adversarially more fragile) or an indifferent addition of complexity, and only when a false-accept costs at least an order of magnitude more than a miss does fusion deliver genuine, simultaneous economic and robustness value — this is, alongside Section 4.3's verdict on building a custom architecture, this paper's second conditional (not unconditional) answer, this time to the question of whether inference-time model fusion is worth deploying.

**Limitations.** (1) Both the training-trajectory sweep and the fusion experiments were run on LmArena only; whether Quora/SearchQueries show the same "complete by 25%" pattern or the same r≈10–20 threshold is untested. (2) The fusion cost-ratio analysis's threshold grid caps at 0.90, so the threshold-tuned-alone baseline is pinned at the grid boundary for r≥100 — the reported fusion gain at the high-r end should be read as directional, not exact. (3) Weighted averaging tested only fixed-weight convex combinations; a more systematic stacking approach (learning a general linear combination on held-out data) was not attempted, constrained by this paper's own rule that the red-team set can never be used for calibration — a proper stacking approach would need a dedicated calibration set not overlapping any published result, which this section does not construct. (4) `(score + 1) / 2` is a mathematically valid but uncalibrated fixed transform; the two component scores are not necessarily on a comparable confidence scale before fusion. (5) The cost-ratio framework is based entirely on the natural test set's hit_rate/error_rate; the redteam set's false-accept rate is reported only as a reference and is not folded into cost(r) itself — this section answers "is fusion worth it for natural-traffic cost," not "what is adversarial robustness itself worth," which would require a dedicated adversarial-robustness cost model this section does not provide. Artifacts: `scripts/qcached_nli_forgetting_sweep.py`, `scripts/qcached_nli_fusion_eval.py`, `scripts/qcached_nli_fusion_cost_analysis.py`, and the corresponding `results/qcached_nli_*.json` files.

### 4.5 The similarity signal the gray-zone gate discards: a causal validation of joint decisions, the boundary of its reliability, and an online adaptive threshold

**Motivation.** Since the main paper's original architecture design (§3.1), the gray-zone gate's final decision has been a cascade: `sim ≥ τ_high` passes directly, `τ_low ≤ sim < τ_high` calls the verifier, and the verifier's score **alone** decides — the similarity score that admitted the request into the gray zone is discarded entirely, never participating in the final verdict. The Top-K cascade's (the main paper) rank-1 decision discards even more: not just rank 1's own similarity, but everything already known about rank 0 (a rejected candidate whose similarity and verifier score were already computed). Similarity and verifier score carry different information and different error patterns — that is the very premise behind adding a verifier at all. By Neyman-Pearson detection theory, a rule that decides on a single signal is a special case of the larger class of rules that decide on a joint region over both signals, and is theoretically weaker than the best achievable joint decision region. This section systematically tests whether that principle cashes in at the architecture's core decision point, and along the way uncovers a more fundamental problem than "does joint decision help."

**Method and results 1: joint decision for the K=1 gray zone — mathematical principle and causal validation.** A 2-feature logistic regression (similarity, verifier score, fit on standardized features) replaces the one-dimensional "verifier score only" rule, re-tracing the hit_rate/error_rate frontier on 5 conditions (LmArena/Quora/SearchQueries, off-the-shelf/fine-tuned verifiers). The first pass confirmed the predicted direction, but a methodological self-audit found the original calibration/test split unit and denominator did not match this paper's own established honest-calibration protocol (the main paper) — the corrected figures were accepted only after exactly reproducing the already-published calibrated threshold (0.6241) and hit_rate/error_rate (0.9823/0.0632) on the LmArena fine-tuned condition:

| Dataset/verifier | Joint-decision hit_rate gain at matched error rate | 95% CI | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| LmArena, off-the-shelf | -1.05pp | [-1.74, -0.54] | Significantly worse |
| LmArena, fine-tuned | -0.09pp | [-0.17, +0.03] | Not significant |
| Quora, off-the-shelf | +0.56pp | [+0.26, +0.64] | Significantly better |
| SearchQueries, off-the-shelf | +1.69pp | [+1.48, +1.85] | Significantly better, largest gain |
| SearchQueries, fine-tuned | +0.17pp | [+0.10, +0.28] | Significantly better |

The direction depends on the dataset: joint decision is significantly worse where the verifier is strong (LmArena) and significantly better where it is weak (Quora/SearchQueries). **A single-dataset controlled experiment ruled out "this is just a dataset-domain confound"**: holding LmArena, the similarity distribution, the labels, and the split protocol fixed, the only variable changed is injecting increasing Gaussian noise into the verifier score — as the injected noise grows and the verifier's own AUC falls monotonically from 0.7385 to 0.5734, the joint-decision gain transitions smoothly from significantly negative (-1.05pp at σ=0) to significantly positive (+2.40pp at σ=3.0), with the crossover landing right in the AUC≈0.60–0.68 range — exactly where Quora's (0.6309) and SearchQueries' (0.5984) real verifier AUCs sit. This rules out text-feature-based alternative explanations and confirms "the weaker the verifier, the larger the joint-decision gain" is causal.

**Method and results 2: searching for a free, calibration-time-only judgment call — four independent attempts, all failed.** The more practical production question is whether, facing a new scenario, one can decide whether joint decision is worth enabling without running a held-out validation. Four progressively more refined attempts all failed to produce a reliable criterion: (1) **A Wald significance test** — the hypothesis that an insignificant similarity coefficient at calibration time predicts no benefit was directly refuted: the coefficient was highly significant (p≈0) in **all** 5 conditions, including the worst-performing LmArena condition (whose coefficient was in fact the largest of any condition) — statistical significance and practical usefulness are different things. (2) **A single internal cross-validation split** (splitting the calibration set itself in half) — 2 of 5 conditions were false positives, both in the smallest-sample, smallest-true-effect scenarios, biased in the dangerous direction (greenlighting deployment where it shouldn't be). (3) **Forward-chaining (blocked, chronological) cross-validation** — splitting by time and predicting only the immediate future from the past, still 3/5 overall, but breaking the results down by fold revealed an internal LmArena trend from "looks helpful" to "actually harmful"; using only the last fold's sign matched all 5/5, but its confidence interval crossed zero — directionally right, not statistically significant. (4) **Recency-weighted (exponentially decaying) fitting** — using all historical data while emphasizing recent rows; the fitted coefficients genuinely shifted with the half-life, but the downstream hit_rate/error_rate frontier barely moved, for a clean mathematical reason: Youden's J plus frontier evaluation depends only on the score's induced **relative ranking**, and this reweighting never crossed the threshold needed to actually reorder validation samples, so naturally the metric couldn't detect any difference.

**Method and results 3: generalizing to the Top-K cascade — a dazzling result that needed debunking.** Applying the same principle to the main paper's K=2 cascade's rank-1 decision, with rank 0's policy held fixed (reusing the already-audited K=1 threshold) and only rank 1's decision rule varied. The first pass: LmArena off-the-shelf showed +21.13pp, the largest gain in this entire line of investigation — large enough to demand immediate suspicion. Investigation found the rank-1-reachable subpopulation (only 8.6% of all requests) has a positive rate climbing from 0% to 83% across the stream — early on the cache is sparse and rank 1 is almost never correct; late on it is dense and rank 1 is often the true duplicate — a structural cache-density effect, not a decision-rule achievement. Switching to forward-chaining brought LmArena's average gain down to +13.38pp, but the per-fold gains (+1.6% → +8.9% → +20.5% → +22.6%) still tracked the positive-rate climb almost in lockstep — forward-chaining only fixes "predicting the past from the future"; it cannot fix the more fundamental problem that the label base rate itself drifts so severely that every validation fold is easier than the fold it was fit on. A further attempt at explicit detrending (adding a stream-position covariate) not only failed to strip out the trend (the gain instead rose to +17.42pp) but also surfaced a second, independent finding: Youden's J itself is fragile under covariate shift (the trend-aware baseline performed worse than the plain baseline on nearly every fold). **LmArena's rank-1 decision gain remains impossible to pin down within this paper's tested methodology.** By contrast, SearchQueries (mild drift, 0.30–0.42) and Quora (mild monotone decline) held up consistently across three progressively stricter tests: SearchQueries went from +7.13pp (single split) to +6.76pp (forward-chaining) to +8.87pp (detrended) — the gain stayed significant and of consistent magnitude throughout, a credible positive result; Quora settled around +1–1.5pp, directionally consistent but small and uncertain.

**Unified reflection: this paper's honest-calibration methodology has, all along, carried an assumption that was never separately tested.** Taken apart, the dozen-plus experiments above are individual positive/negative results; taken together, they point at the same wall. This paper's honest-calibration methodology (chronological calibration/test split + Youden's J threshold selection) implicitly assumes the **ranking** induced by the verifier's score is stable between the calibration window and the deployment window — a different claim from "the optimal threshold itself might drift" (the main paper's already-tested-and-corrected finding), and this paper had only ever tested the latter. Everything in Methods and results 1/2 — when joint decision helps (is the ranking already sufficient?), why the Wald test fails (statistical significance ≠ reordering validation samples), why recency weighting shows no effect (rank invariance) — is the same "is the ranking stable" question wearing different clothes. All three static-threshold remedies in Methods and results 3 (single split, forward-chaining, explicit detrending) failed on LmArena for the same reason: they all still operate within the paradigm of "refit a threshold on one window, apply it to the next," whose validity depends, start to finish, on the assumption that ranking or threshold transfers statically. **This is not an isolated new finding — it is an independent rediscovery, from a completely different experimental design, of the same wall Section 4.2 already published**: CRC's per-request validity gate fails on real streams because the verifier score distribution's covariate shift is hard for simple reweighting/recalibration methods to capture and correct. This is the second time, independently, from two entirely different experimental designs, that this paper has landed on the same core lesson.

**Method and results 4: switching to a tool that does not assume static ranking/threshold transfer — an online adaptive threshold cleanly resolves the problem.** With static-threshold remedies already ruled out four different ways, the natural next step is an online adaptive threshold in the spirit of Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI, Gibbs & Candès 2021): with no offline split at all, processing requests in their original stream order, maintaining a threshold `θ_t`; at each step, decide `score_t ≥ θ_t`, observe the true label, and if the decision was an accept-and-wrong (`err_t=1`) raise the threshold (more conservative), otherwise lower it slightly (more permissive), with the update `θ_{t+1} = θ_t + γ(err_t - α)` proportional to how far the realized error rate deviates from the target α (0.05 here) — a direct implementation of ACI's "correct the target level online from real-time observations" control-loop idea. The control is a static baseline (the same warm-up window calibrates a threshold once, then it is frozen).

| Condition | Static baseline hit_rate/error_rate | ACI hit_rate/error_rate | Mean deviation from target (static→ACI) |
|---|---|---|---|
| LmArena, verifier score | 2.53% / 0.28% (target 5%) | **21.35%** / 3.48% | 0.047→0.020 |
| LmArena, joint fused score | 10.77% / 1.33% | **38.81%** / 3.66% | 0.038→0.018 |
| SearchQueries, verifier score | 0.11% / 0.05% | **9.23%** / 4.95% | 0.050→0.005 |
| SearchQueries, joint fused score | 0.10% / 0.03% | **14.12%** / 4.94% | 0.050→0.006 |

The static baseline badly wastes its error budget on both datasets: the threshold calibrated on the warm-up window is extremely harsh and, once frozen, realizes an error rate far below target — at the cost of a hit rate too low to be usable. **After ACI's online adjustment, SearchQueries' error rate tracks the target almost exactly, and hit rate rises 80–140x; LmArena, despite tracking less precisely given the more severe drift, still tracks the target far better than a frozen threshold, with hit rate rising 8–9x.** Switching to the joint fused score pushes hit rate further up on both datasets (LmArena 21%→39%, SearchQueries 9%→14%) while tracking precision, if anything, improves — confirming the unified reflection's hypothesis: "ranking quality" (joint decision) and "threshold adaptivity" (ACI) are two independent, stackable levers.

**Conclusion.** The cascade architecture's never-before-questioned choice to discard the similarity signal does have real, observable headroom, confirmed both theoretically (Neyman-Pearson) and causally (the noise-injection dose-response) — but that headroom only opens when the verifier's own discriminative power is insufficient; when it is already strong, adding a dimension instead hurts, due to finite-sample estimation noise. It is not a free architectural upgrade — it is a conditional benefit that requires first diagnosing verifier strength, and that diagnosis itself has no free shortcut (four attempts, all refuted). Generalizing the principle to the Top-K cascade exposed an even more fundamental problem: the population reachable by later cascade candidates can carry severe non-stationarity tied to cache-growth dynamics, under which no "fit a static threshold, then deploy" protocol — however finely split — can be trusted or well-calibrated, regardless of split sophistication; this diagnosis is the same coin as Section 4.2's independently published CRC covariate-shift finding, seen from the other side. The real fix is not further refinement of threshold calibration, but switching to an online adaptive framework that does not assume static transferability of ranking or threshold — ACI beats the static baseline decisively in both severe drift (LmArena) and mild drift (SearchQueries) without needing to diagnose which regime applies beforehand, and its benefit stacks with joint decision's. This upgrades Section 4.2's coarse recommendation of a "forced recalibration cadence" into a per-request, online-corrected refinement backed by a formal coverage-guarantee principle.

**Limitations.** (1) The economics of joint decision (when is it worth deploying) were only worked out for the K=1 gray zone (Section 4.4's fusion cost-ratio framework); the same cost-ratio framework was not extended to the Top-K cascade or the ACI setting, so the three "is it worth it" judgments are currently three independent lines of evidence, not unified into one framework. (2) The four "free shortcut" attempts rule out only the four specific methods tested here, not the existence of any viable calibration-time diagnostic. (3) The Top-K cascade's LmArena-specific gain figure was never pinned down to a trustworthy point estimate within this paper's tested methodology — this is evidence that "the current methods cannot measure it," not that "joint decision is ineffective on LmArena"; the two should not be conflated. ~~(4) The ACI implementation is a direct, principle-level adaptation, not a literal reproduction of the cited paper's regression-interval formalism; no rigorous derivation was done of under what assumptions this specific adaptation retains a formal long-run coverage guarantee — the results reported are empirical, not a new theorem.~~ [Closed in Section 4.6, which turns its existing finite-sample proposition into a deployment-scale diagnostic, plus two corollaries; see that section.] (5) ACI's step size, warm-up window size, and other hyperparameters were not swept for sensitivity. ~~(6) ACI was only validated on the Top-K cascade's rank-1 decision, not wired back into this paper's core Group D/E gray-zone decision framework for an end-to-end test.~~ [Section 4.6 brings ACI back to the K=1 gray-zone CRC risk-control framework (SearchQueries, Quora), though only that CRC-calibrated variant, not the plain (non-CRC) Group D/E hit_rate/error_rate frontier.] Artifacts and full record: `scripts/joint_grayzone_decision_analysis_v2.py`, `scripts/joint_grayzone_doseresponse_causal_test.py`, `scripts/joint_grayzone_calibration_time_pretest.py`, `scripts/joint_grayzone_internal_cv_holdback.py`, `scripts/joint_grayzone_forwardchain_holdback.py`, `scripts/joint_grayzone_recency_weighted_holdback.py`, `scripts/topk_cascade_joint_decision_analysis.py`, `scripts/topk_cascade_forwardchain_analysis.py`, `scripts/topk_cascade_detrended_analysis.py`, `scripts/aci_online_threshold_cascade.py`, and the corresponding `results/*.json` files.

### 4.6 Bringing ACI back to the problem it was meant to solve, and giving it a finite-sample formal guarantee

**Motivation.** Section 4.5 validated an online adaptive threshold (ACI) as superior to static-threshold remedies on one side branch, the Top-K cascade's rank-1 decision, but left two things unfulfilled: (1) Section 4.5's conclusion asserted ACI "upgrades Section 4.2's coarse 'forced recalibration cadence' recommendation into a refined version," but ACI was never actually used to solve the original problem that motivated citing Paper B (formal risk control) §4.1 in the first place — SearchQueries' real, documented CRC guarantee violation; (2) ACI's implementation was repeatedly flagged as "a direct principle-level adaptation, with no rigorous derivation of a formal guarantee." This section closes both gaps — but it's worth being upfront about what "closing" means here: the finite-time bound in Method and results 3 follows the same telescoping-plus-bounded-iterate argument as the original ACI paper's own finite-sample guarantee (Gibbs & Candès 2021); this section does not claim a new proof technique. What it actually contributes is applying that argument to this paper's specific threshold-space ACI setting, distilling it into a deployable, unit-free **window-scale diagnostic** (`ρ = W/W*`), and then honestly testing (Method and results 4) what that diagnostic can and cannot predict.

**Method and results 1: back to the original problem — can ACI fix the CRC guarantee violation documented in Paper B (formal risk control) §4.1?** Data and split reuse the already-published Protocol T pipeline exactly (SearchQueries, corrected: all four α levels {0.05, 0.02, 0.01, 0.005} exceed target under a chronological split). The CRC-calibrated threshold from the first half becomes ACI's starting point, adapted online over the second half in stream order, using CRC's own 0/1 risk definition — no additional held-out data beyond what the original protocol already used:

| α | Static CRC overall risk (vs. target) | Static windows exceeding (of 10) | ACI overall risk | ACI windows exceeding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 0.0623 (25% over) | 10/10 | **0.0500** | 7/10 |
| 0.02 | 0.0239 (20% over) | 10/10 | **0.0200** | 4/10 |
| 0.01 | 0.0121 (21% over) | 9/10 | **0.0100** | 4/10 |
| 0.005 | 0.0061 (22% over) | 7/10 | **0.0050** | 4/10 |

The static threshold overshoots by a stable 20%–25% across all four α levels, with nearly every rolling window exceeding target. After ACI's online adjustment, overall risk lands within a tiny margin of target at all four levels (relative deviation dropping from 0.012–0.013 to 0.0003–0.0005), and windows exceeding target drop from a 7–10/10 static baseline to 4/10 at most α levels — but α=0.05, the default level used everywhere else in this paper, only drops to 7/10, a markedly weaker improvement than the other three levels. To be clear: ACI does not achieve zero violations — depending on α, 4/10 to 7/10 windows still briefly exceed target, an inherent oscillation of the online control loop, not a hard coverage guarantee; the hit-rate cost is modest (from 16.4% to 13.4% at α=0.05). The same pipeline applied to Quora (whose Protocol-T violation Section 4.2 already showed is a QQP labeling batch effect, not real online drift) shows a similar improvement — ACI does not distinguish "real production drift" from "systematic trends baked into how the data was constructed"; it tracks and corrects for any systematic shift in the score-label relationship along the stream, which is both its power and a boundary worth keeping in mind.

**Method and results 2: the first test of ACI against real production drift (rather than a benchmark dataset) — a different, and sensible, result.** Every (query, answer) row in the real comcastcares Twitter customer-support brand's gray-zone stream (the main paper) was scored with the off-the-shelf verifier (this dataset has no precomputed score cache like the benchmarks do), using the exact split point the main paper's actual Group E fine-tuning used (2,579-row training window, 1,106-row held-out traffic). At three α levels, ACI's overall risk showed no clear advantage over the static baseline (slightly worse at α=0.05/0.02, identical at α=0.01). A 100x sensitivity sweep on the step size (1%–100% of the calibration score's standard deviation) left the risk stable within the same narrow band throughout, ruling out step-size mistuning as the explanation — this is an honest boundary finding, not a bug to be hunted down.

**Method and results 3: a finite-time formal bound, and the window-scale diagnostic derived from it.** ACI's specific update rule, `θ_{t+1} = θ_t + γ*(err_t - α)` (`err_t = 1{accept and wrong}`), admits a direct proof:

> **Proposition.** Let `(score_t, label_t)`, `t=1..T`, be an arbitrary sequence (no i.i.d. or stationarity assumption required — adversarial drift is covered), `γ > 0`. If `θ_t in [θ_min, θ_max]` for all `t=1..T+1`, then `|(1/T) sum(err_t) - α| <= (θ_max - θ_min) / (T*γ)`.

**Proof** telescopes the update rule term by term: `θ_{T+1} - θ_1 = γ * sum(err_t - α)`, rearranged to `(1/T)sum(err_t) - α = (θ_{T+1}-θ_1)/(T*γ)`, then bounded using θ's boundedness, giving what we call the **ACI-specific bound**. The same argument holds for any sub-window `[t+1, t+W]` (replace `T` with `W`): `|(1/W) sum(err_s) - α| <= (θ_max-θ_min)/(W*γ)`.

This ACI-specific bound can be combined with a second, simpler fact that does **not** depend on the update rule at all: since `err_t in {0,1}`, `0 <= (1/W) sum(err_s) <= 1` directly, so `|(1/W) sum(err_s) - α| <= max(α, 1-α)` holds for the windowed average of *any* `{0,1}`-valued sequence — a **trivial binary-risk range bound**, unrelated to ACI's update rule or `γ`. Taking the minimum of the two gives a tighter window bound:

```
| (1/W) sum(err_s) - α | <= min( (θ_max-θ_min)/(W*γ), max(α, 1-α) )
              ACI-specific bound (tightens with W)     trivial range bound (independent of W)
```

The two terms are equal at `W* = (θ_max-θ_min) / (γ * max(α, 1-α))`: below `W*`, the trivial range bound is what's actually active — the guarantee doesn't get worse as the window shrinks further, but it also never tightens as the window grows, and carries essentially no information about whether the calibration process itself is working; above `W*`, the ACI-specific bound is active and genuinely tightens as the window grows. (The update rule separately implies that each step moves `θ_t` by at most `γ * max(α, 1-α)` — this fact is not the source of the trivial range bound, but it is useful for the boundedness discussion in Limitation (1) below.)

`W*` motivates a dimensionless **window-scale diagnostic**, `ρ = W/W*`: `ρ >> 1` means the ACI-specific bound is active and tightening; `ρ < 1` means the bound is pinned at the trivial range bound. Re-checking this bound against all 11 configurations tested in Methods and results 1/2 (SearchQueries and Quora at 4 α levels each, comcast at 3), it holds in every case, and `ρ` locates where each scenario sits relative to the crossover:

| Scenario | Sample size `T` | Window size `W` | Crossover `W*` | `ρ = W/W*` | Regime |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SearchQueries | 43,140 | 4,314 | ~130 | ~33.2 | `ρ >> 1`, ACI-specific bound active |
| Quora | 8,520 | 852 | ~161 | ~5.3 | `ρ >> 1`, likewise |
| comcast | 1,106 | 184 | ~156 | ~1.18 | `ρ ~ 1`, right at the crossover |

(comcast's sample size is far smaller than the other two datasets, so this table computes `W` over 6 rolling windows rather than the 10 used for SearchQueries/Quora, `W = 1,106 / 6 ~ 184`; this is a "typical window" diagnostic for the comcast data, and is not the same window convention as the `ρ ~ 7.11` reported separately in Method and results 4, which treats the entire 1,106-row held-out stream as a single window — that figure refers to the one actual, non-repeatable deployment originally reported in the main paper. The two should not be conflated.)

comcast's real deployment window sits almost exactly at this crossover (`ρ ~ 1.18`) — **three claims of different strength need to be kept separate here, not conflated**: (1) `ρ` diagnoses whether the theoretical bound itself still carries force — this follows directly from the derivation above, it is a mathematical fact, not an empirical claim that data could overturn; (2) "`ρ ~ 1` is a universal threshold at which ACI's empirical advantage disappears" is a separate, much stronger empirical claim — Method and results 4 tests it directly; (3) "`ρ > 1` implies ACI empirically beats a static baseline" is a third, still stronger claim that the proposition never implies and that this section is not designed to test. comcast's single data point only speaks to claim (1) — the theoretical bound is indeed near-vacuous at this scale, in quantitative agreement with Method 2's "no clear ACI advantage" — and a single data point cannot upgrade that agreement into claim (2)'s causal or universal reading. The bound remains a directly usable tool for pre-deployment sizing on claim (1): to keep the risk deviation over a window of length `W` within a tolerance `ε` (with `ε < max(α, 1-α)`, otherwise any window length already trivially satisfies it), it suffices to have `W >= (θ_max-θ_min) / (γ * ε)` — no need to actually deploy and measure, just compare the expected traffic window against this formula.

**Method and results 4: closing the loop with an actual controlled experiment — directly sweeping the window length to test whether crossing ρ=1 really predicts ACI's advantage vanishing.** Holding the same `λ0` (CRC-calibrated threshold), `γ`, and `α=0.05` fixed throughout, the only variable swept is how long a stretch of historical traffic one "redeployment" of ACI covers: each dataset's test stream is chopped into non-overlapping windows of a given length, static (frozen `λ0`) and ACI (`θ_0=λ0`, fresh restart) are each run independently in every window, and the mean "ACI advantage" (static deviation minus ACI deviation) at that window length is aggregated with its standard error. SearchQueries and Quora are long enough to sweep `ρ` from about 0.12 all the way past 300, crossing the theoretical crossover from both sides; comcast's 1,106-row test segment caps `ρ` at about 7.1, and the number of repeatable non-overlapping windows shrinks as the window grows (at `ρ ~ 7.1` only one window remains — the single real deployment already reported).

![Figure 1: ACI's advantage swept across ρ -- left: static vs. ACI risk deviation (log scale), with the dashed line showing the theoretical bound shared by all three datasets; right: ACI's advantage over static, error bars are the across-window standard error, stars mark single, non-repeatable windows (comcast's actual deployment is one of them)](results/aci_window_sweep_causal_test.png)

The result **does not cleanly support the "crossing ρ=1 predicts the advantage vanishing" hypothesis — the truth is more complicated**: on SearchQueries and Quora, ACI's advantage is significantly positive across the entire swept range, including well below `ρ=1` — SearchQueries already shows `+0.0052 +/- 0.0009` at `ρ ~ 0.15`, Quora `+0.0020 +/- 0.0007` at `ρ ~ 0.12`. The advantage does grow in magnitude with `ρ`, but it never shows a kink or drops to zero around `ρ=1` — the hypothesis is directly falsified on both of these datasets. comcast shows a similar shape within the range its own data allows — growing with `ρ`, significantly positive around `ρ ~ 1-4` (e.g. `+0.0046 +/- 0.0011` at `ρ ~ 1.86`) — and only falls back toward zero or negative at the two largest window lengths (`ρ ~ 4.17` and `~7.11`, each with only 1 non-overlapping window left), one of which (`ρ ~ 7.11`) is exactly the single real deployment already reported. With only one window, there is zero statistical power to distinguish "the advantage systematically vanishes as the window grows" from "this one specific slice of comcast's history happens to be unusual" (the main paper already documented that this brand's gray-zone positive rate keeps drifting, non-stationarily, throughout its history).

**This further tightens the causal language used above**: the quantity that more directly drives ACI's typical advantage appears to be the number of online-adaptation steps available itself, not whether the proposition's worst-case bound has tightened (`ρ`'s position relative to 1) — the two are correlated but not the same thing. Even when `ρ` is far below 1 and the theoretical bound carries almost no information, as long as the static threshold carries a systematic bias (exactly what a CRC violation is), even a few dozen adaptation steps can capture part of that bias and produce a real advantage. The more accurate description of comcast's "no advantage" finding is "this one specific, non-repeatable historical window happened to land in a weak-advantage region," not "the window was too short for the theoretical guarantee to mean anything, so the advantage disappeared" — `ρ` and `W*` remain valid and useful for claim (1) above, but claim (2) (`ρ ~ 1` as a universal empirical-advantage-disappearance threshold) is the specific, stronger hypothesis this experiment falsifies, not claim (1) itself, which is a mathematical fact unaffected by this data.

**Identification limitation.** This window sweep cannot cleanly identify whether empirical ACI advantage is associated with the absolute deployment-window length `W` or with the normalized window length `ρ = W/W*`. Across the three scenarios, the estimated crossover lengths `W*` are of similar magnitude (approximately 130, 156, and 161 observations), so varying `W` necessarily induces a highly correlated variation in `ρ`. The present experiment can test whether the proposed crossover is consistent with empirical behavior, but cannot establish that normalization by `W*` provides explanatory power beyond raw window length. Separating these hypotheses would require scenarios with substantially different values of `W*` — for example through materially different `γ`, threshold-range width `θ_max - θ_min`, or target error level `α` — ideally (as an experimental-design goal, not a mathematical requirement) spanning at least an order of magnitude in `W*`; no such dataset is available here.

**Two corollaries formally connect this proposition to Section 4.5's joint-decision findings.** First (score-invariance): the proposition's proof never used any specific meaning of `score_t`, only that `accept_t` is defined by one comparison — so swapping in any score function (the raw verifier score, Section 4.5's joint fused probability, or anything else) leaves the risk-control guarantee intact, with only the constant `(θ_max-θ_min)` changing to that score's own range. Second (the hit-rate ceiling is governed by the ROC curve): fixing a risk budget `α <= 1-π` (`π = P(Y=1) in (0,1)` is the population correctness rate), the highest hit_rate a single score function `s` can achieve is `hit_rate*(s, α) = π * ROC_s(α/(1-π)) + α` (attained at the constraint boundary `FPR_θ = α/(1-π)`; the proof is a standard monotonicity argument — hit_rate is non-decreasing in `FPR_θ`, so push it to the allowed maximum). If two score functions' ROC curves satisfy pointwise dominance (Section 4.5's causal noise-injection experiment validated exactly this: the joint fused score dominates the lone verifier score when the verifier's own discriminative power is insufficient), the hit-rate ceiling is pointwise dominated too.

These two corollaries require **asymmetric** assumptions, and that asymmetry is the final mathematical resting point of this paper's entire "ranking stability vs. threshold stability" thread: the risk-control proposition needs no population/ranking stability assumption at all; the hit-rate-ceiling proposition needs `π` and `ROC_s` to be roughly stable over the window under consideration. This precisely explains a whole series of previously only empirically-supported phenomena: why the four "free shortcut" attempts in Section 4.5 all failed (they were fundamentally trying to estimate, from finite calibration samples, whether one score function's population-level stability/dominance holds — inherently noisy); why LmArena's Top-K rank-1 decision resisted even forward-chaining and explicit detrending (the rank-1-reachable population's positive rate itself drifts severely, breaking the hit-rate-ceiling proposition's premise outright, while the risk-control proposition is entirely unaffected and ACI can still be deployed there); and why joint decision and ACI compose seamlessly in Section 4.5 (they correspond to two propositions whose assumption sets never overlap).

**Conclusion.** This section's contribution is not a new telescoping proof technique — Method and results 3's argument follows the same style as the original ACI paper's own finite-sample guarantee (Gibbs & Candès 2021) — but a deployment-scale interpretation of that existing guarantee: a normalized window-scale diagnostic, `ρ = W/W*`, computable before deployment, plus a controlled window sweep that separates "is the theoretical bound informative" from "does ACI actually provide empirical advantage over a static threshold." The bound is distribution-free, requiring none of the exchangeability assumption CRC's original finite-sample theory (Paper B §4.1) depends on and which has already been shown to fail under chronological splits. But Method and results 4 shows `ρ` reliably answers only the first of those two questions: comcast's lack of observed advantage is more likely a property of that one specific historical window than a systematic effect of window length, and `ρ ~ 1` is not a universal threshold for empirical advantage disappearing. Read together with Section 4.5's causal findings, the entire investigation in this paper — from the single original observation that the cascade architecture discards the similarity signal, through "when does this principle hold, why can't the calibration stage detect it, why does it break in a different scenario, and what tool fixes it once it breaks" — converges on two complementary mathematical propositions plus one honest falsification, not a single tidy formula that explains every experiment; the latter story would have been harder to defend, and the former is more credible for it.

**Limitations.** (1) The proposition's hypothesis "`θ_t` is bounded" was only empirically verified for the actual (unprojected) implementation used here — recomputing the full `θ_t` trajectory for two scenarios at one α level each confirmed it stayed well away from the observed score range's boundaries — not guaranteed by construction; a rigorous guarantee requires switching to the projected update rule `θ_{t+1} = Π(θ_t + γ*(err_t - α))` (`Π` is projection onto `[θ_min, θ_max]`), which changes nothing for the scenarios already run (since `θ_t` never touched the boundary anyway) but makes the proposition hold unconditionally for any future deployment. (2) The proposition is a worst-case bound; real data (including comcast's genuine drift) shows an observed deviation one to two orders of magnitude smaller than the theoretical bound throughout — the proposition guarantees "no worse than," not "how good in the typical case," and Method and results 4 confirms this directly: `ρ` governs how much force is left in the theoretical bound, not the size of the empirical advantage, which visibly decouples from `ρ` on SearchQueries/Quora (significantly positive even at `ρ << 1`). (3) The hit-rate-ceiling proposition is fundamentally a local/short-term statement (depending on `π` and `ROC_s` staying roughly constant within the window considered), with no quantitative characterization of how much the ceiling itself would drift under slow population drift. (4) The hit-rate-ceiling proposition gives only an upper bound, not a characterization of what fraction of that ceiling an online method like ACI actually achieves in practice — welding the risk-control proposition and the hit-rate-ceiling proposition into a single result jointly constraining both is a larger theoretical goal not attempted here. (5) The ROC-dominance relationship (joint decision beating the lone verifier score) was only causally validated, in Section 4.5's empirical results, under the specific condition "verifier discriminative power insufficient" — it is not a general property holding for any two score functions, and must be separately confirmed, as Section 4.5 did, before applying the dominance corollary. (6) Method and results 4's window sweep tested only `α=0.05`, and every window's ACI run restarted fresh from `θ_0=λ0` (one specific redeployment assumption, not "a continuously running, never-reset ACI instance"); comcast has only 1 non-repeatable window at its largest tested lengths, so "this is a property of that specific historical window, not a systematic window-length effect" is currently the more plausible reading, not a proven conclusion — it needs replication on other real datasets with similarly non-stationary drift. (7) The three datasets' crossover lengths `W*` are of similar magnitude (~130–161), so sweeping `W` is nearly equivalent to sweeping `ρ` by a fixed rescaling — this experiment cannot establish that normalizing by `W*` provides explanatory power beyond raw window length; separating the two hypotheses needs scenarios with substantially different `W*`. ## 5. Discussion

Six sections of results look, on the surface, like six independent tests; taken together they point at the same wall. This paper's honest-calibration methodology (and the main paper's and Paper B's) implicitly assumes that the ranking a verifier score induces is stable between the calibration window and the deployment window — a claim this paper is the first to test in isolation. Sections 4.1 and 4.2's negative results, why all four of Section 4.5's "free shortcuts" fail, and why the Top-K cascade cannot be reliably measured, are all different manifestations of this same assumption being violated. Section 4.2 independently hits a covariate-drift problem from the angle of CRC validity, and Section 4.5 independently hits a candidate-pool non-stationarity problem from the angle of the decision rule — two completely different experimental designs converging on the same core lesson, which is itself some of this paper's most persuasive evidence.

What matters most for a deployer is that this paper's negative and positive results are two sides of the same piece of advice: do not look for a remedy at the *per-request* granularity (neither uncertainty-based abstention nor a per-request validity gate can, structurally, beat a simple global policy); the levers that actually work are global and periodic — a mandatory recalibration cadence plus a distribution-drift alarm — and a free improvement that needs no retraining, just correctly feeding an existing signal back into the decision rule (joint decision). When the candidate pool's own non-stationarity is severe enough that a static-threshold protocol cannot be measured reliably at all, the only clean solution is to switch to an online adaptive threshold — and Section 4.6 shows this tool, too, can carry a distribution-free, finite-sample guarantee, without giving up formal guarantees in exchange for adaptivity.

Section 4.6's final finding — that the window-scale diagnostic `ρ` can reliably answer only "does the theoretical bound still carry force," not "does ACI have an empirical advantage" — is this paper's most important methodological self-correction. This paper did not package the window-sweep experiment into a tidy story of "one formula that explains every experiment"; it honestly reports the process of a hypothesis being falsified. That does not weaken Section 4.6's contribution — it makes it more credible.

---

## 6. Limitations

Beyond the limitations each section already lists, three cross-cutting limitations apply across the whole paper: (1) of this paper's six results sections, only Section 4.6 was tested against real production drift data (comcastcares); the other five rest entirely on trace-driven simulation over three benchmark datasets, and the limitation the main paper and Paper B have already repeatedly noted on this point applies equally here; (2) Sections 4.1 and 4.2's negative results, and Sections 4.3–4.6's positive results, hold only for the combination of this paper's three tested datasets and specific verifier architectures (the ms-marco family and the NLI family introduced via Paper C); whether these conclusions generalize to other architectures or other domains' datasets is untested; (3) this paper does not unify Sections 4.3–4.5's joint-decision gains, Section 4.6's ACI gains, Paper B's cost-ratio framework, and Paper C's adversarial-robustness findings into a single deployment-decision framework covering every dimension — these remain several independent lines of evidence that a practitioner currently has to weigh and combine manually, rather than a ready-made formula.

## 7. Conclusion and Future Work

This paper systematically tests several paths for extracting more value without retraining the verifier, finding two negative (uncertainty-based abstention, a per-request CRC validity gate) and one positive (joint decision); extending the positive result further locates a more fundamental non-stationarity problem — the candidate pool's composition can drift severely as the cache grows, which no static-threshold protocol can handle. This paper resolves that problem with an online adaptive threshold (ACI), supplies a distribution-free, finite-sample formal proposition for this application, and honestly tests — and partly falsifies — what that proposition's diagnostic quantity can actually predict. The shared lesson across all six sections: the implicit "ranking/threshold static stability" assumption behind semantic-cache reuse decisions deserves just as much scrutiny as the already-tested question of "does the threshold itself drift" — and an online adaptive framework, not a more finely tuned static calibration, is the right class of tool for this kind of non-stationarity.

Future work, in priority order:

1. Test whether Sections 4.1 and 4.2's negative results generalize to ensemble disagreement and conformal nonconformity — two signal classes genuinely designed for epistemic uncertainty — rather than being confined to the "score proximity" class of signals this paper tested.
2. Unify Sections 4.3–4.5's joint-decision gains and Section 4.6's ACI gains with Paper B's cost-ratio framework into a single deployment-decision framework, replacing the three currently independent lines of evidence.
3. Repeat Section 4.6's window-sweep experiment on other real production datasets with non-stationary drift, to test whether "comcast showed no empirical advantage because of that one historical window" holds up.
4. Supply a projected-update-rule implementation for Section 4.6's proposition (`θ_{t+1} = Π(θ_t + γ*(err_t - α))`), upgrading boundedness from an empirical check to a guarantee by construction.
5. Test whether Section 4.6's reuse-rate-ceiling proposition and risk-control proposition can be welded into a single result jointly constraining both.
6. Using scenarios with substantially different `W*` (ideally differing by at least an order of magnitude), test whether the window-scale diagnostic `ρ` itself provides explanatory power beyond the raw window length `W` — this paper's three datasets have similar `W*` values and cannot answer this identification question.
