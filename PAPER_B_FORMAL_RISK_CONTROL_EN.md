## Abstract

The main paper grounds the threshold choice for gray-zone reuse decisions in honest calibration (chronological split + Youden's J), but that is still only a point estimate — it never answers how much confidence the resulting error-rate figure deserves given a finite calibration sample. This paper connects Conformal Risk Control (CRC; Angelopoulos et al., 2024) to that decision, producing a distribution-free risk guarantee that holds exactly at finite sample sizes, and honestly stress-tests whether that guarantee survives two realistic deployment conditions: (1) whether calibration and test samples are genuinely exchangeable — when calibration and test are split chronologically, across three datasets only one dataset's guarantee still holds (and conservatively so), while the other two overshoot: one statistically stable, consistent in direction, and severe in magnitude (all four alpha levels exceed target, with relative overshoot reaching 80% at the tightest level), the other mild (roughly 20-25%); this paper treats the specific cause of the former (real production drift, versus an artifact left by the dataset's own construction) with caution and does not resolve it; (2) whether the reuse decision itself feeds back into future cache composition, inducing a self-selection feedback loop — a genuine online closed-loop simulation confirms this effect is real, and it follows a precise monotonic dose-response relationship with the dataset's own direct-hit rate: the higher the direct-hit rate, the larger the harm the feedback loop causes; online recalibration fully compensates for this harm on two datasets, but not on the dataset with the highest direct-hit rate. Finally, this paper proposes an explicit cost-ratio economic framework answering under what cost structure synchronous verification is actually worth deploying: when the cost of an error clearly exceeds the cost of a miss (typically r≳ 1-9, dataset-dependent), the mechanism wins economically almost throughout; only when the two costs are close, or misses are even more expensive, is a static threshold aimed straight at hit rate the better choice. Together, these three sections turn "giving cache reuse a mathematical guarantee" from an abstract theoretical promise into a deployable tool with concrete usage boundaries and an economic decision rule attached.

## 1. Introduction

The main paper shows that whether synchronous verification gating realizes its theoretical benefit under a real verifier depends heavily on how well the verifier matches the business data domain, and that in-domain fine-tuning is currently the only remedy validated to work. But that conclusion rests on a point estimate: the threshold honest calibration selects corresponds to one specific error-rate number, and the main paper never quantifies how much confidence that number itself deserves — if a customer asks "can you guarantee my error rate stays under 1%," the main paper's answer can only be "we measured X% on a held-out test set," not a promise that carries a confidence guarantee.

Conformal Risk Control (CRC; Angelopoulos et al., 2024) was designed to answer exactly this kind of question: it generalizes split conformal prediction to any bounded, monotone, right-continuous loss function, producing a distribution-free guarantee that holds exactly at finite sample sizes. This paper connects that method directly to the semantic cache's gray-zone reuse decision, testing whether "should this hit be reused" can be upgraded from a point estimate with no quantifiable confidence into a decision with a user-specified risk budget and a formal guarantee.

But a formal guarantee is not free — it depends on specific mathematical preconditions (the most important one here being that calibration and test samples are exchangeable), and real deployment environments are naturally prone to violating exactly this kind of precondition: traffic drifts over time, and if the reuse decision itself affects which candidates remain in the cache in the future, the calibration distribution can even be shaped by the decision's own history. This paper is therefore not satisfied with merely proving the guarantee exists, and goes on to ask two harder, more honest questions: does this guarantee still hold under real, non-stationary traffic order? Does it still hold if the decision itself feeds back to shape the future calibration distribution? And even where the guarantee holds, when is this mechanism actually worth deploying economically?

## 3. Experimental Setup (Reused from the Main Paper; Restated Only as Necessary)

**Datasets.** This paper uses all three of the main paper's datasets — SemCacheLMArena (60,000 conversational records), SemCacheSearchQueries (150,000 short-query records), and Quora Question Pairs (roughly 60,000 paraphrase-detection records) — full construction details in the main paper's Section 4.1.

**Gray-zone architecture.** When a query's similarity to a cached candidate falls in `[tau_low, tau_high)`, the request enters the gray zone and is handed to the verifier to decide whether to reuse; this paper fixes tau_high=0.97, tau_low=0.80, both the original anchors used throughout the main paper.

**Honest-calibration protocol.** The main paper's honest calibration splits each dataset's traffic chronologically into a first half (calibration) and second half (test), selects a threshold via Youden's J on the calibration half, and evaluates on the test half — Section 4.1's Protocol T reuses this same split point directly.

**Four experimental groups.** This paper reuses the main paper's naming — Group A (static similarity threshold, no verifier call), Group B (a line-by-line port of vCache's `VerifiedDecisionPolicy`, learning a threshold online per cache entry), Group C (an oracle verifier using ground truth to decide approval, representing the theoretical ceiling), Group D (a real, non-fine-tuned off-the-shelf cross-encoder verifier), Group E (the same cross-encoder fine-tuned on each dataset's own gray-zone labels); full definitions and implementation details are in the main paper's Sections 3 and 4.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 From Point Estimates to Finite-Sample Risk Control: Calibrating the Gray-Zone Reuse Decision with Conformal Risk Control


**Motivation.** The main paper's honest calibration (chronological calibration/test split, Youden's J threshold selection) solves the problem of whether a threshold is peeking at the test set, but the threshold it selects is still just a point estimate — it never answers how much confidence the resulting error-rate figure deserves given a finite calibration sample. Conformal Risk Control (CRC; Angelopoulos et al., 2024, arXiv:2208.02814) generalizes split conformal prediction to any bounded, monotone, right-continuous loss function, giving a distribution-free guarantee that holds **exactly at any finite sample size**: E[L_{n+1}(λ̂)] ≤ α. This section connects that method to the gray-zone verifier threshold, testing whether the decision to reuse a cached hit can be upgraded from a point estimate with no quantifiable confidence into a decision with a user-specified risk budget and a formal guarantee — not bolting a confidence interval onto the existing error-rate figure, but replacing threshold selection itself with a calibration procedure that takes a risk budget as input.

**The quantity being controlled.** Among the existing metrics, `error_rate` (used throughout Section 5) already has the right *shape* — an unconditional per-query loss, P(incorrect ∧ hit), with the denominator over all requests — it just isn't scoped to the gray zone; `verifier_fidelity` is already gray-zone-scoped but is conditional (fp/(tp+fp)). What CRC actually needs to control is the intersection of the two, which had no standalone implementation before this section:

R_GZ(λ) = P(incorrect ∧ accept_λ | gray zone)

The new `gray_zone_risk_curve` (`cacheverifier/metrics/core.py`) computes this curve. **Implementation detail:** the accept rule must be strictly right-continuous (accept_λ(x) := score(x) > λ, not ≥) to satisfy the precondition of CRC's Theorem 1 — under an inclusive rule, a tied candidate lands on the wrong side of its own discontinuity. This detail has no observable effect on any number reported elsewhere in this paper (continuous cross-encoder scores essentially never tie), but it is a necessary condition for the formal guarantee to hold, not a stylistic choice; the first implementation's "subtract the point's own index" approach gave two candidates tied at the exact same score two different risk values, caught in the act by a synthetic test (`tests/test_crc.py::test_b_tied_scores_use_strict_inequality_not_inclusive`) and fixed with `searchsorted`.

**The CRC selector** (matches Theorem 1, eq. 4 in Angelopoulos et al., 2024):

λ̂ = inf{ λ : (n/(n+1))·R̂_n(λ) + B/(n+1) ≤ α }

where n is the calibration sample size and B bounds the loss (B=1 in this paper, since R_GZ is a 0/1 loss). **When the candidate set is empty, the algorithm itself defines λ̂ := λ_max** (reject the entire gray zone) — this is not a failure mode: under this paper's construction, L_i(λ_max)=0 holds exactly for every calibration point (no candidate's score can strictly exceed the sample's own maximum), so the guarantee still holds even along this fallback path, and it should never be described as "CRC being infeasible." This condition degenerates into a dataset-independent closed-form floor — n ≥ ⌈1/α − 1⌉ (for B=1): α = 5%/2%/1%/0.5% correspond to 19/49/99/199 respectively. All three datasets clear this floor by 4×–217× at every calibration size actually used (the tightest case is Quora's existing 909 at τ_low=0.95; at the τ_low=0.80 band this section uses, the three datasets have 8,051/8,520/43,140 respectively) — sample size was never the reason reuse was limited in this section's experiments.

**Protocol R and Protocol T.** CRC's guarantee requires calibration and test samples to be exchangeable. The gray-zone candidates' similarity retrieval is itself stateful — match quality shifts systematically as the cache grows (see the drift audit below) — so a chronological split (the "first half calibration, second half test" convention the main paper's honest calibration already uses) does not satisfy this assumption. But for a fixed, already-realized finite pool of samples — regardless of what process generated it — a uniform random split makes the calibration set exchangeable with any test point, purely by the symmetry of the randomization itself; this is the standard justification for split conformal methods and requires no stationarity assumption whatsoever about the underlying generating process. This section therefore runs two parallel protocols: **Protocol R** (uniform random split, supporting the strict claim "resampling uniformly from this same fixed benchmark pool," a formal CRC result) and **Protocol T** (chronological split, a deployment-transfer/drift-robustness experiment, explicitly not an application of the CRC theorem and not a claim about real production traffic).

τ_high is fixed at 0.97; τ_low is fixed at 0.80 (the widest value in the grid, matching the verifier scores already cached, requiring no new model inference).

**Protocol R results** (50/50 random splits on each dataset, M=200 independent repeats, reporting mean realized risk, its 95% CI, the single-run exceedance probability P(risk>α), mean reuse, and efficiency relative to the full-data empirical oracle, η = U_CRC/U_oracle):

| Dataset | AUC (untuned baseline) | n_calibration | α=5% | α=2% | α=1% | α=0.5% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LmArena | 0.720 | 8,051 | risk 0.0499, reuse 68.98%, η 0.999 | risk 0.0200, reuse 40.67%, η 1.001 | risk 0.0100, reuse 21.32%, η 1.002 | risk 0.0050, reuse 9.87%, η 1.022 |
| Quora | 0.631 | 8,520 | risk 0.0499, reuse 10.01%, η 0.997 | risk 0.0199, reuse 4.34%, η 0.996 | risk 0.0100, reuse 2.37%, η 1.000 | risk 0.0050, reuse 1.21%, η 0.985 |
| SearchQueries (corrected) | 0.598 | 43,140 | risk 0.0498, reuse 13.99%, η 0.999 | risk 0.0199, reuse 6.10%, η 0.997 | risk 0.0100, reuse 3.24%, η 0.999 | risk 0.0050, reuse 1.67%, η 0.997 |

(Youden's J's own single-split reference point is reported separately, since it is not alpha-targeted: LmArena mean_test_risk=0.0509, Quora 0.2806, SearchQueries 0.2095 — these three numbers differ enormously, but that only reflects each gray zone's own base rate of incorrectness, not a signal about CRC's calibration quality.)

On all three datasets, at all four alpha levels, mean_risk sits almost exactly on target — not conservatively below it. This is by design: the CRC selector picks the loosest λ satisfying the constraint, so it should hug the boundary rather than leave safety margin. **Precisely because of this, roughly half of single runs will show a realized risk slightly above α (P(risk>α) ranges 0.435–0.525 across the four levels) — this is what a tightly (but correctly) bound guarantee looks like, not under-coverage; the E[L] ≤ α constraint bounds the mean, not any individual realization.** η lands within 3% of 1.0 across all three datasets and all four alpha levels, and the dataset with the largest calibration sample (SearchQueries, 43,140) sits closer to 1.0 than the two smaller ones (LmArena/Quora, roughly 8,000–8,500) — **at the sample sizes tested here, CRC's finite-sample guarantee costs almost no utility relative to an oracle that knows the full distribution, and that cost shrinks further with more calibration data, exactly as theory predicts.**

**Headline finding: AUC ranking does not predict risk-constrained operational-utility ranking.** SearchQueries has the lowest AUC (0.598) of the three datasets, yet its reuse rate is higher than Quora's (AUC 0.631) at **every one of the four alpha levels** — 1.67% vs. 1.21% at α=0.5%. A verifier that ranks worse by AUC can still be operationally more useful under a fixed false-reuse risk budget. This is not a hypothetical illustration — it is an actual rank reversal measured across this section's three datasets, and it is closer to what a deployer actually cares about ("can this verifier be used under this specific risk constraint") than reporting an AUC number alone: AUC measures ranking quality, CRC reveals risk-constrained operational utility, and the two are not the same thing.

**Protocol T results** (chronological split, single run, first half calibration / second half test, the same split point the main paper's honest calibration uses):

| Dataset | calibration-half incorrect rate | test-half incorrect rate | ratio (test/cal) | exceeds α at all four levels? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LmArena | 0.4088 | 0.0574 | 0.140 (test much easier) | No — conservative at all four, none exceed |
| Quora | 0.5511 | 0.6563 | 1.191 (test harder) | **Yes — exceeds at all four, up to 80% relative overshoot at α=0.5%** |
| SearchQueries (corrected) | 0.5147 | 0.5170 | 1.005 (net drift nearly cancels) | Yes, but mild (roughly 20–25% relative overshoot) |

This result is directly explained by the direction gray-zone composition drifts over stream position, quantified with an audit that needs no new inference — just binning the already-cached trace into deciles of stream position: LmArena's gray-zone incorrect rate falls from 63.5% at decile 0 to 5.0% at decile 9; Quora runs the opposite direction, rising from 43.6% to 67.6%; SearchQueries rises then falls, and its net effect happens to nearly cancel at exactly the 50/50 split point. LmArena's drift direction happens to favor chronological calibration (calibrating on the harder first half and applying it to the easier second half is naturally conservative); Quora's drift runs the opposite way, calibrating on the easier first half and systematically under-preparing for the harder second half — all four alpha levels exceed target, in the same direction, and this is statistically stable (not sampling noise), qualitatively different from Protocol R's roughly 50% single-run exceedance rate (where the guarantee still holds, just tightly bound).

**But whether this statistically stable overshoot corresponds to real production drift is something this section treats with caution and does not resolve**: Quora's "stream order" is the order in which question pairs first appear in the QQP training file, not any independently verified real timestamp — this "drift" could reflect genuine, time-accumulated difficulty change, or it could just as easily be some systematic artifact left by the dataset's own construction/annotation process (crowdsourced labeling can differ systematically across batches, for instance); this section did not independently verify which explanation holds, so **this result should not be described outright as a confirmed case of "CRC failing under real production drift."** The more conservative, more defensible statement is: treating a threshold calibrated on this dataset's given order as carrying a CRC-theorem guarantee for traffic outside that order is not safe on data like Quora's — this holds regardless of how the "is the drift real" question is eventually answered, and that narrower open question is left to future work.

**vCache methodology audit: the conclusion is not to attempt a direct numeric comparison.** The main paper already discusses Group B in detail (this paper's line-by-line port of vCache's `VerifiedDecisionPolicy`, `cacheverifier/cache/adaptive_threshold.py`); to judge whether its `target_error_rate` and this section's CRC α can be compared numerically, that port's implementation was re-examined, and vCache's own abstract was checked to confirm it "learns an optimal threshold for each cached prompt." Three structural mismatches, any one of which alone would be enough to make a direct numeric comparison meaningless:

1. **Decision granularity.** vCache maintains a separate threshold **per individual cache entry**, updated online as that entry accumulates its own observation history (min_observations=6); this section's CRC calibrates **one global threshold over the entire pooled gray zone**. "vCache's error rate" is really an average over many entry-level policies at different stages of maturity; "CRC's certified risk" is a single number for one shared policy.
2. **Verification timing.** vCache's `observe()` only updates an entry's history on a miss (the explore branch). This is **asynchronous** verification: a hit is trusted unconditionally in the moment, and verification, when it happens, only informs future decisions. This section's gray-zone gating is **synchronous**: it decides before serving the current hit. `target_error_rate` and this section's α describe guarantees under two different causal structures, not two settings of the same dial.
3. **Guarantee type.** vCache's explore probability τ comes from a fitted one-dimensional logistic regression of correctness on similarity, plus a delta-method/normal-approximation confidence bound — a parametric, asymptotic-flavored construction. CRC (Theorem 1) is distribution-free and exact at any finite n. Comparing "vCache's guaranteed error rate" against "CRC's certified risk" would be comparing an approximate, model-dependent bound to an exact, model-free one — not the same kind of claim even before asking what population each is defined over.

This section therefore does not report "vCache's error rate is X%, CacheVerifier's CRC-certified risk is Y%, ours is better/worse" — any apparent numeric edge could just as easily reflect one of these three mismatches as a real difference in mechanism quality. The defensible framing is structural, filled in here with verified facts rather than placeholders:

| Dimension | This section's method (CRC) | vCache |
|---|---|---|
| Decision granularity | one global threshold over the pooled gray zone | per-cache-entry threshold |
| Verification timing | synchronous (gates the current hit) | asynchronous (only on miss/explore, informs future decisions) |
| Guarantee type | distribution-free, exact, finite-sample (CRC Thm 1) | parametric (logistic + normal approximation), asymptotic-flavored |
| Calibration | offline, batch calibration/test split | online, per-entry, incremental |
| Cold start | product side already has documented warnings (cacheverifier-service) | `min_observations=6` per entry, explicit cold-start miss branch |
| Adapts to local difficulty | no (this section's Quora/SearchQueries reuse ceiling is exactly this limitation showing up) | in principle yes, calibrated per cached prompt |

A genuine head-to-head, if ever wanted, would need new engineering on one side or the other — reimplementing this paper's gray-zone gate as a per-entry asynchronous policy, or reimplementing vCache's decision rule under a single pooled synchronous threshold — not simply running the two existing implementations against each other. That is scoped as a separate, explicitly unfinished future direction, not part of this section's conclusions.

**Conclusion.** This section upgrades the gray-zone reuse decision from a point estimate with no quantifiable confidence into a decision with a user-specified risk budget and an exact finite-sample guarantee under a random-split assumption, and that guarantee costs almost no utility at the sample sizes tested here (8,051–43,140; η within 3% of 1.0 throughout). This upgrade surfaces two facts that were never quantified before: first, a verifier's AUC ranking does not equal its operational-utility ranking under a specific risk budget (SearchQueries turns a lower AUC into higher reuse than Quora); second, calibrating on historical traffic and deploying to future traffic — an operation nearly every production system performs — is only as safe as the traffic's own exchangeability, two of the three datasets show overshoot (Quora statistically stable, consistent in direction, and severe in magnitude — whether the specific cause is a systematic artifact left by the dataset's construction or real production drift was not independently verified in this section; SearchQueries mild in magnitude but consistent in direction), suggesting this cannot be assumed to hold by default. Sample size itself, at the scales tested in this section, was never the reason reuse was limited — the limit comes from the verifier's own discriminative power, consistent with the main paper's diagnosis, just now expressed in the formal language of a risk budget.

### 4.2 The CRC Gate's Self-Selection Feedback Loop: A Genuine Online Closed-Loop Test


**Motivation.** Section 4.1's Protocol R and Protocol T both test static splits of a fixed, already-completed historical trace (random vs. chronological order) — neither is a genuine closed loop. If the CRC gate's own reuse/reject decisions feed back into future cache state — approved candidates persisting and getting hit more, rejected ones getting silently crowded out of the candidate pool — that could induce a self-selection feedback loop: the calibration distribution itself gets systematically shaped by the verifier's own past decisions, not just drifting naturally with time. This was hypothesized to potentially be a stronger failure mode than the exchangeability violation Protocol T already demonstrated, but it had never been tested in isolation.

Before designing this test, a more basic premise was checked first: **every single experiment** the main paper, this paper's Section 4.1, and the main paper's Top-K cascade depend on inherits one convention from vCache's own benchmark harness: every request gets inserted into the cache **unconditionally**, regardless of whether it was a hit or a miss. That means the candidate pool's composition at any point in the stream has never actually depended on any policy's history of decisions — the self-selection mechanism this section tests has never been simulated anywhere before. Not "not yet finished testing" — "never started."

**Implementation.** A new script, `scripts/crc_closed_loop_self_selection.py`, does not modify the main paper's existing experimental framework and instead implements a genuine online closed loop from scratch. The same record stream is run through three regimes in sequence:

```
baseline:          insert every record regardless of hit/miss
                    (= the convention the main paper uses)
                    + threshold calibrated once from an initial warm-up
                      slice, then frozen
self_select:        insert only on miss (a hit reuses; no duplicate write)
                    + threshold fixed at baseline's own calibrated value
self_select_recal:  insert only on miss
                    + threshold re-derived online via crc_select_threshold:
                      every 500 new gray-zone observations, recalibrate
                      from the most recent 2,000 (sliding window)
```

`self_select` changes exactly one variable relative to `baseline` (write semantics) — the threshold value is identical, transplanted as a plain number from `baseline`'s own calibration rather than re-derived on `self_select`'s own data — so any difference between the two can be attributed to "does cache content get shaped by its own decisions" alone, not to the threshold also happening to differ. `self_select_recal` adds a second variable (online recalibration) on top of `self_select`, testing whether recalibration can compensate for whatever damage self-selection causes. Sanity check: `baseline`'s Quora gray-zone candidate count (17,040) matches the same existing static trace cache file used in this paper's Section 4.1 exactly, digit for digit — confirming the new harness faithfully reproduces the existing mechanism rather than being an independent implementation that might carry its own bugs.

Regime comparisons use only post-warm-up observations (`baseline` alone goes through a warm-up period where every gray-zone candidate is rejected under a fixed fail-closed policy — matching `cacheverifier-service`'s own real cold-start default — before its threshold is calibrated; `self_select`/`self_select_recal` use the transplanted, already-calibrated threshold from their very first observation and never go through this warm-up period. Including the warm-up period in the comparison would pull `baseline`'s overall risk down artificially via this zero-risk stretch, producing a spurious difference unrelated to the self-selection mechanism itself). Risk differences use an unpaired percentile bootstrap (`bootstrap_risk_difference`, 2,000 resamples, 95% CI; once two regimes diverge, their matched candidate sequences are no longer the same, so there is no natural way to pair observations one-to-one — this is deliberately unpaired, not paired).

**Quantitative results** (alpha=0.02; "direct-hit rate" and "cache sparsification" are both taken from `baseline`/`self_select`'s actual runs):

| Dataset | Direct-hit rate | self_select final cache / baseline | Self-selection alone (self_select vs. baseline) | Online recalibration (self_select_recal vs. self_select) | Recalibration vs. original baseline |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quora | 3.7% | 56,912/60,000 (5.1% fewer) | diff=−0.0001, CI [−0.0036,+0.0034] **not significant** | diff=−0.0055, CI [−0.0090,−0.0021] significant improvement | diff=−0.0057, CI [−0.0091,−0.0023] **beats the original baseline** |
| SearchQueries (corrected) | 28.1% | 102,929/150,000 (31.4% fewer) | diff=+0.0068, CI [+0.0050,+0.0085] **significantly harmful** | diff=−0.0195, CI [−0.0211,−0.0180] significant improvement | diff=−0.0127, CI [−0.0142,−0.0112] **beats the original baseline** |
| LmArena | 72.8% | 13,586/60,000 (77.4% fewer) | diff=+0.0305, CI [+0.0274,+0.0337] **strongly significant, risk more than triples** | diff=−0.0239, CI [−0.0270,−0.0206] significant but incomplete improvement | diff=+0.0065, CI [+0.0038,+0.0093] **still significantly worse than the original baseline** |

The three datasets sit precisely on one monotonic dose-response curve, not three unrelated dataset quirks: the higher the direct-hit rate (i.e., the larger the share of queries similar enough to an existing cache entry that the verifier is never even consulted), the more a write-on-miss-only policy — the semantics that more closely match a real production system — sparsifies the cache, and the larger the harm self-selection alone causes. Quora (3.7%/5.1%) shows no detectable effect; SearchQueries (28.1%/31.4%) causes a moderate, significant harm; LmArena (72.8%/77.4%) causes a strongly significant harm that more than triples realized risk. This is the first real, measured confirmation of the self-selection closed-loop mechanism anywhere in this paper — not a theoretical hypothesis anymore.

One non-monotonic wrinkle is worth recording honestly. Online recalibration fully compensates for self-selection's harm on both Quora and SearchQueries — even beating the original baseline — but not on LmArena, where recalibration still leaves a significant residual harm relative to the original (non-self-selecting) baseline. This suggests that once the harm magnitude clears some threshold, recalibration alone is no longer sufficient to fully offset it; that threshold sits somewhere between SearchQueries' 28% and LmArena's 73% direct-hit rate, but the three datasets merely happened to land on either side of it rather than being designed to locate it — its exact value remains an open question.

**Locating this threshold: write probability instead of synthetic data construction, interpolating directly inside LmArena.** Two earlier approaches to locating this threshold were considered and rejected. (1) Sweeping τ_high to artificially raise or lower a dataset's direct-hit rate — rejected, because τ_high isn't just a measure of "how much redundancy the traffic itself contains," it is also the boundary at which similarity alone is trusted without the verifier (the main paper already showed moving τ_high is never a neutral operation); an effect measured this way would confound "changing self-selection exposure" with "the direct-hit shortcut itself becoming less reliable," not a clean single-variable manipulation. (2) Injecting synthetic exact-duplicate records into a low-redundancy dataset (e.g. Quora) to artificially pull its direct-hit rate up — rejected, because this introduces a new "is synthetic duplicate data realistic enough" debate, requires more engineering, and doesn't directly use the one real dataset already shown to exhibit the effect.

The final choice instead generalizes `run_regime`'s binary switch (`insert_on_hit`, whether to write on a hit) into a continuous parameter `insert_on_hit_probability` — on a hit, the cache is still rewritten with probability p (p=1 is the original baseline, p=0 is `self_select`, and intermediate values correspond to a real policy such as "occasionally rewrite popular entries," e.g. a TTL-driven periodic refresh). This keeps the embedding model, verifier, τ_low/τ_high, and threshold calibration protocol all unchanged — the only manipulated variable is the rewrite probability on a hit — requires no synthetic data construction, and this parameter itself directly corresponds to a real, implementable product mitigation, not just a diagnostic knob.

On LmArena (reusing the already-calibrated `frozen_threshold`, not re-derived), the "self-selection + online recalibration" protocol was run once for each p ∈ {0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75}, compared against the original baseline via an unpaired bootstrap:

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 (already tested) | 0.0203 | +0.0065 | [+0.0038,+0.0093] | significant residual harm |
| 0.1 | 0.0198 | +0.0060 | [+0.0031,+0.0088] | significant residual harm |
| 0.25 | 0.0190 | +0.0052 | [+0.0025,+0.0080] | significant residual harm |
| 0.5 | 0.0186 | +0.0049 | [+0.0021,+0.0076] | significant residual harm |
| 0.75 | 0.0179 | +0.0042 | [+0.0013,+0.0070] | significant residual harm |

Harm does decrease monotonically with p — that trend is real — but **it decreases slowly**: even at p=0.75 (the cache is still rewritten on three out of every four hits, already fairly close to the original baseline's write intensity), residual harm relative to p=0 has fallen by only about 35% (from +0.0065 to +0.0042), and the effect never leaves the significant range anywhere across the tested interval [0, 0.75]. This is a more pessimistic but more honest conclusion than expected: **"occasionally rewrite popular cache entries" as a mitigation for self-selection harm is far less of a bargain than it might seem — fully offsetting the residual harm on a high-redundancy stream like LmArena may require p very close to 1, i.e., essentially giving up the write-efficiency gain self-selection itself was supposed to provide**, rather than finding a "small cost, large benefit" sweet spot. Exactly where between p=0.75 and 1.0 the effect actually stops being significant (if it does) was not tested further this round and remains an open question.

**Qualitative confirmation.** A statistical pattern alone doesn't confirm a mechanism, so every false-accept in `self_select` (a gray-zone request the verifier approved but whose candidate was actually wrong) was audited case by case, across **all three datasets**. Method: search backward through the stream for that query's true match (an earlier record sharing the same equivalence_id), and check whether that true match's own occurrence was ever written into `self_select`'s store (i.e., whether it itself ever experienced a miss).

| Dataset | Direct-hit rate | Total post-warm-up false-accepts | First occurrence (unrelated to self-selection) | Crowded-out share of explainable cases |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LmArena | 72.8% | 1,098 | 58 (5.3%) | **68.9%** (717/1,040) |
| SearchQueries (corrected) | 28.1% | 3,667 | 2,081 (56.7%) | 12.4% (196/1,586) |
| Quora | 3.7% | 432 | 353 (81.7%) | 15.2% (12/79) |

On LmArena, of 1,098 post-warm-up false-accepts, 58 were genuine first occurrences (no earlier equivalent to compare against, unrelated to self-selection); of the remaining 1,040 explainable cases, 717 (**68.9%**) fall under "every earlier equivalent record was itself a hit, never independently written" — precisely matching the hypothesized mechanism: a correct candidate, by virtue of repeatedly being successfully reused, never gets its own chance to be written into the cache, so later queries of the same kind are forced to fall back on some topically-similar-but-not-actually-equivalent substitute — one that happens to still fool the cross-encoder's score. The manually inspected examples confirm this is not a statistical coincidence: LmArena contains large recurring clusters of well-known "viral" trick-question templates, the same puzzle rephrased a dozen-plus different ways. `self_select`'s cache retains only a handful of canonical entries per cluster to absorb all future hits.

**This specific mechanism does not transfer directly to SearchQueries and Quora, which is the most important scope-narrowing finding of this round of auditing.** SearchQueries does show a significant overall self-selection harm (see the table above), but crowding-out explains only 12.4% of its explainable cases, far below LmArena. More notably, 56.7% of SearchQueries' false-accepts have no earlier equivalent record at all — the large majority are simply the query's first occurrence, a verifier judgment problem in its own right, largely unrelated to the self-selection mechanism. Quora is similarly dominated by first occurrences (81.7%), consistent with its own self-selection effect already being non-significant overall, and its sample of explainable cases (only 79) is too small to draw a strong conclusion from either way. **Conclusion: the specific "a correct candidate gets crowded out of the cache by its own repeated successful reuse" mechanism is mainly a phenomenon of high-redundancy traffic like LmArena's; the self-selection harm that genuinely exists on SearchQueries is mostly explained by a different mechanism this round did not identify** — plausibly related to a systematic decline in HNSW approximate-retrieval recall quality as the overall candidate pool composition shifts, rather than any one specific candidate being targeted for crowding-out, but this is an unverified conjecture, not something this audit itself can answer.

**Limitations.** (1) Only alpha=0.02 was tested, unlike Section 4.1's Protocol R/T four-level sweep. (2) The warm-up window (1,500 gray-zone observations), recalibration cadence (every 500), and sliding-window size (most recent 2,000) are all modeling choices with no sensitivity analysis; the write-probability sweep itself reused the same three fixed values and did not test whether they would change how slowly this "compensation" decreases. (3) The write-probability sweep was only run on LmArena, not repeated on SearchQueries (which had already fully compensated at p=0, leaving no residual harm to sweep against); the sweep also only reached p=0.75 — the true crossover point (if one exists) between 0.75 and 1.0 was not located further. (4) The finding that "crowding-out explains most of LmArena's harm but not most of SearchQueries'" is currently only a result of elimination (this one mechanism was ruled out; what actually dominates SearchQueries' harm was not found or verified). (5) Like the main paper, this remains trace-driven simulation, not a genuine online closed loop in a real production environment — real systems' specific implementation details around "does a hit trigger a fresh write" (e.g., an independent eviction policy, TTLs) could further change the magnitude of effect measured here.

### 4.3 A Cost-Sensitive Reanalysis: Beyond the Hit-Rate/Error-Rate Frontier, Under What Conditions Is Synchronous Verification Actually Worth It


**Motivation.** Every result table elsewhere in this paper reports a hit-rate/error-rate Pareto frontier and leaves it to the reader to informally judge whether a given point is "worth it" — a framing that implicitly treats every false-accept and every miss as equally costly, which is never true in a real deployment: a miss just costs one extra LLM call, while a false-accept costs customer trust, refunds, or support escalation — usually a much larger and harder-to-quantify number, and the ratio between the two varies by business. Rather than continue reporting a frontier curve, this section treats the cost ratio r = C_false\_accept / C_miss as a sweepable parameter, directly answering: over what range of r is synchronous verification economically worth it?

**Method.** No new experiments are needed — this reuses existing Group A (static threshold, 9 grid points) and Group D/E (honest calibration, from the main paper, not the original grid-searched numbers) hit_rate/error_rate values. For each dataset:

```
cost(r) = r × error_rate + (1 − hit_rate)        [in units of C_miss]
```

gives the expected cost of each operating point at a given r (derivation: the higher hit_rate, the more misses are saved; the higher error_rate, the more false-accept cost is paid, both converted into the same currency, C_miss). For each group, the operating point minimizing cost(r) is that group's best performance at that r; comparing D/E's best against A's best determines which is economically superior. A fine log-spaced grid (r ∈ [0.01, 1000], roughly 100 points per decade) is scanned to find every r-range where D/E wins — a few representative r values would not suffice, since "which method wins" as a function of r is not necessarily monotonic (each group's best-achievable cost is the lower envelope of several linear functions of r, piecewise-linear and concave; the difference between two such curves is not guaranteed to cross zero only once, so a coarse grid could miss an intermediate winning window). Script: `scripts/cost_sensitive_reanalysis.py`; results: `results/cost_sensitive_reanalysis.json`.

**A grid-coverage issue the first run exposed, and how it was closed.** Group D/E's honest calibration had originally only been run at tau_low ∈ {0.80, ..., 0.95} (6 points), while Group A's static-threshold grid covers up to 0.99 (9 points). The first run showed all three datasets "losing back" to A at large r — but exactly where this reversal occurred coincided with the range where D/E's grid has no operating point as conservative (as low-error) as A's tau=0.99, leaving it unclear whether this was a genuine economic reversal or purely a grid-coverage artifact. To rule this out, the main paper's honest-calibration scripts were rerun at tau_low ∈ {0.97, 0.98, 0.99}, tau_high=0.999 (D and E, all 3 datasets, 6 runs total), and the 3 newly produced operating points were concatenated onto the original 6-point files, bringing D/E's grid to the same 9 points as Group A's. This step was anomalously slow on local CPU for a reason not fully diagnosed — measured at roughly 3 ex/s, which would take hours per dataset; moving to a remote GPU server (Tesla T4) restored throughput to 80–113 ex/s, with all 6 jobs finishing sequentially in about 30 minutes.

**Results — after closing the grid gap, the conclusion is stronger and cleaner than the first pass:**

| Dataset | r-range where D wins | r-range where E wins |
|---|---|---|
| LmArena | **[0.71, ∞)** (tested up to 1000) | **[0.28, ∞)** |
| Quora | [5.01, ∞) | [2.00, 2.82] ∪ [5.01, ∞) |
| SearchQueries (corrected) | [1.41, 3.98] ∪ [8.91, ∞) | [1.26, 4.47] ∪ [7.08, ∞) |

Once the grid was closed, the earlier "A reverses back on top at large r" phenomenon on LmArena **disappeared entirely** — confirming it was a grid-coverage artifact, not a genuine economic reversal; after closing the gap, D/E win essentially across the entire tested range (r≳0.3–0.7 onward), no longer needing a qualified "middle sweet spot" framing. Quora (where D previously "never won") and SearchQueries (where the high-r range previously appeared to lose) both win back at sufficiently large r once the grid is closed, showing a two-window pattern (a narrower mid-range winning window plus an unbounded winning region at high r). All three datasets now share the same qualitative conclusion: **when the cost of an error clearly exceeds the cost of a miss (typically r≳1–9, dataset-dependent), synchronous verification is economically superior to a static threshold almost universally**; only when miss and false-accept costs are close, or misses are even more expensive (small r), is a static threshold aimed straight at maximum hit rate the better choice — consistent with the naive intuition that "the more expensive errors are, the more valuable a verification mechanism becomes," a direction the pre-grid-fix data had briefly appeared to overturn at the high-r end, now confirmed to have been an artifact.

**Limitations.** (1) The cost ratio r is a free parameter; this section never estimates a real r value for any actual business scenario — every range answers "if r falls in this range," not a claim about where any specific company's r actually sits. (2) cost(r)'s linear form assumes costs simply add, with no scale effects or nonlinear penalties (e.g., the trust collapse from accumulating multiple false-accepts is plausibly not linear) — this is a simplifying model, not one derived from real business cost data. (3) Group C (oracle) appears in the table only as a reference ceiling, not included in the D/E-vs-A win/loss determination, since an oracle is not a deployable policy. (4) D/E honest calibration gives exactly one operating point per tau_low (chosen via Youden's J on the calibration half), unlike Group A's per-threshold sweep — this is an existing limitation of the main paper's honest-calibration methodology itself, not something newly introduced here. (5) The newly added grid points (tau_low=0.97–0.99) used underlying score values from a different range in the local vs. GPU environment (the same phenomenon independently discovered and diagnosed in a companion paper — the adversarial-robustness red-teaming paper — see that paper for details), but since Youden's J calibration and testing both use scores from the same internally-consistent run, this does not affect the hit_rate/error_rate numbers this cost analysis actually depends on.

**[2026-09-10 update, checking a claim the commercial code cites but this section never actually tested]** The commercial service `cacheverifier-service`'s `verifier_core/finetune.py::threshold_by_cost_ratio` — the production function that picks the cost-minimizing threshold from calibration data at a given r, the direct product-side implementation of this section's cost(r) framework — has a docstring claiming the threshold moves through "often a wide, non-monotonic-boundary region rather than a single sharp cutover point" as r varies, citing this section as the source. But this section never tested that: this section answers "which strategy — A vs D/E — is more economical at a given r" (win/loss regions between strategies), while that citation describes a different question — **how the specific threshold `threshold_by_cost_ratio` picks, on one verifier's own score curve, moves as r varies** — this section never produced an answer to that question; it is a case of code citing a claim this paper never verified.

Using `threshold_by_cost_ratio`'s exact algorithm unmodified (sweeping `cost(r,k) = r·fp(k)/n + (n-k)/n` over every candidate cut point k, taking the minimizer), on the calibration half of the main paper's honest-calibration protocol (tau_low=0.80, tau_high=0.97, the same operating point as the rest of this section) — for Group D on LmArena, Quora, and SearchQueries (corrected), plus Group E on LmArena and SearchQueries (corrected) (Quora's Group E checkpoint was never persisted, see the erratum at the top of the main paper, so it is absent) — the threshold curve was recomputed on this section's own grid convention (r ∈ [0.01, 1000], a 500-point log grid, ~100 points/decade):

| Condition | Distinct thresholds within r∈[1,20] | Median plateau width in that range (decades) | Share of the tested range covered by "mega-plateaus" (>1 decade) outside it |
|---|---|---|---|
| LmArena / Group D | 32 | 0.015 (threshold changes roughly every 4% move in r) | 2 mega-plateaus, 3.67/5 decades total |
| LmArena / Group E | 25 | 0.030 (~7%) | 1, 1.99/5 |
| Quora / Group D | 26 | 0.005 (~1%) | 2, 4.32/5 |
| SearchQueries (corrected) / Group D | 27 | 0.010 (~2%) | 2, 4.43/5 |
| SearchQueries (corrected) / Group E | 55 | 0.010 (~2%) | 1, 1.99/5 |

**The claim is misleading, not simply wrong.** Mega-plateaus genuinely exist, and in striking volume — 1–2 per condition, each spanning nearly 2 full decades, together eating up 2 to 4.4 of the 5 tested decades; taken as an aggregate, "often a wide plateau" is literally defensible. But those mega-plateaus fall **entirely** in r < 1 or r ≳ 5–20 (out to the tested ceiling of 1000) — exactly the range where this section's own win-region finding (r≳1–9, dataset-dependent) has already settled the answer, not where a business actually needs to agonize over its precise r. The genuinely decisive window — r in the single digits to the teens, the range most businesses' plausible guesses would fall into — is exactly where the threshold changes most densely: 25 to 55 distinct thresholds packed into a little over one decade, with a median plateau width of only a 1%–7% multiplicative step; the selected threshold has already changed by the time r moves a few percentage points. In other words, the plateaus are real, but they sit in the region where the answer is already clear and precision doesn't matter; in the region where a business actually needs to produce a credible r, threshold selection is quite sensitive — there is no "a rough direction is good enough" shortcut available.

**Limitations**: (1) each condition used a single calibration/test split (an existing limitation shared with much of this section's and the main paper's analysis) — the exact breakpoint locations (which r value is the switchover point) could shift under a different random/temporal split, but the qualitative shape ("wide at the tails, dense in the middle") held consistently across 5 independent conditions (3 datasets × up to 2 verifier groups), making it unlikely to be an artifact of one particular split; (2) only tau_low=0.80/tau_high=0.97 was tested, not the full tau_low grid — whether the plateau structure holds the same shape at other tau_low values is unknown; (3) Quora's Group E is absent due to the missing checkpoint, so whether that dataset is as dense in r∈[1,20] as the other two can only be inferred from its Group D result; (4) this measures the sensitivity of *which threshold gets picked* to r, not *how much hit_rate/error_rate actually differ* between the picked thresholds — the latter is what a business actually cares about as "the cost of guessing r wrong," and this section does not make that conversion; a window that is threshold-sensitive but produces only small hit_rate/error_rate swings could have a milder real-world business impact than the raw sensitivity numbers suggest. Artifacts: `realworld_integration_test/scripts/cost_ratio_plateau_check.py`, `realworld_integration_test/data/cost_ratio_plateau_check.json`.

## 5. Discussion

Together, the three results sections form a complete loop: "giving the reuse decision a mathematical guarantee" splits into three layers. The first (Section 4.1) is whether the guarantee itself holds up — it does under a random-split assumption, and at almost no efficiency cost. The second (Sections 4.1 and 4.2) is what form real deployment pressure on that assumption takes — chronological drift and the self-selection feedback loop are two independent sources of pressure; the severity of the former depends on the direction the traffic itself drifts and on how this data was constructed, while the severity of the latter is monotonically related to the dataset's own direct-hit rate. The third (Section 4.3) is that even when the guarantee holds, whether it's worth the reuse-rate cost depends on the business's own error-cost structure.

One overall lesson worth emphasizing: this paper's two stress tests (Section 4.1's Protocol T and Section 4.2's self-selection closed loop) did not produce a blanket negative conclusion along the lines of "the CRC guarantee collapses the moment it meets real conditions" — instead, they produced specific, actionable boundary conditions: Quora's drift needs its cause interpreted with caution, and LmArena's self-selection harm cannot be fully compensated once the direct-hit rate is high enough. Boundary conditions of this kind are exactly the information needed most when productionizing this mechanism — they flag when extra attention is needed, rather than declaring the whole mechanism unreliable in blanket terms.

Questions this paper does not answer — the specific form this guarantee's failure would take under real, non-stationary production drift (rather than a historical benchmark dataset), and whether there is a better remedy for the self-selection feedback loop's harm in its worst-case scenario beyond "raise the rewrite probability" — are left for the companion paper testing joint decisions and non-stationarity adaptation to pursue further.

---

## 6. Limitations

Beyond the limitations already listed in Sections 4.1, 4.2, and 4.3, three cross-cutting limitations apply across the whole paper: (1) all three sections build on the main paper's existing three datasets; whether the CRC framework, the self-selection closed loop, and the cost-ratio analysis themselves generalize to business scenarios beyond this paper's datasets is unverified; (2) Section 4.1's Protocol T and Section 4.2's self-selection closed loop each test one independent source of pressure; this paper does not test whether the two occurring together (traffic that is both genuinely drifting and self-selecting) compounds the harm, or in what way; (3) all experiments in this paper remain trace-driven simulation, not a genuine online deployment in a real production environment — the end of Section 4.2 already notes this limitation's effect on the self-selection closed-loop results, and it applies equally to all three of this paper's sections.

---

## 7. Conclusion and Future Work

This paper upgrades the semantic cache's gray-zone reuse decision from the main paper's point estimate into a decision with a user-specified risk budget and a formal guarantee, and honestly tests two independent failure paths for that guarantee under real deployment pressure (chronological drift, the self-selection feedback loop), finally giving an explicit cost-ratio framework answering "when is it worth deploying." The three results sections converge on a shared lesson: a formal guarantee is not something that, once proven, can be shelved and forgotten — it depends on specific mathematical preconditions that real deployment conditions can challenge, and needs continued boundary testing, not a static result that, once proven, can be trusted indefinitely.

Future work, in priority order:

1. Have an independent judge (e.g., a different LLM) relabel Quora's gray-zone candidates stratified by stream position, to test whether the error-rate gradient driving Section 4.1's overshoot survives independent relabeling — this is the direct, low-cost next step for determining whether that overshoot is real production drift or a dataset-annotation batch effect; this paper only raises the question and does not carry out this check.
2. Test the specific form the CRC guarantee's failure takes under real, continuous production traffic (rather than the historical benchmark datasets used here), and whether a per-request protection mechanism, triggered only when necessary, can close this gap without sacrificing reuse rate.
3. Locate whether a more economical remedy exists for the self-selection feedback loop, beyond "raise the write probability," in the scenario with the highest direct-hit rate.
4. Test whether Section 4.1's chronological drift and Section 4.2's self-selection feedback loop, occurring together, compound the harm, and in what way.
5. Estimate at least one real-world value for the cost-ratio framework's r parameter using actual business data, rather than treating it purely as a free parameter as this paper does.
6. Test whether cost(r)'s linear-additivity assumption holds in real business scenarios, or whether it needs to be replaced with a nonlinear cost model.
7. Extend this section's vCache structural-comparison table into a genuine head-to-head numeric experiment, which requires new engineering (reimplementing this paper's synchronous global-threshold gate as a per-entry asynchronous policy, or the reverse).
